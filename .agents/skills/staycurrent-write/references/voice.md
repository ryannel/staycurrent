# Voice

Write for a curious engineer. They are intelligent and interested, but may be new to
this subject. Help them follow each step until they can reason about it and make useful
decisions. Every choice below serves that reader.

## Read the writing references

Before planning the explanation or drafting a new piece, read these four Randall
Munroe essays in full:

- [Relativistic Baseball](https://what-if.xkcd.com/1/)
- [A Mole of Moles](https://what-if.xkcd.com/4/)
- [Glass Half Empty](https://what-if.xkcd.com/6/)
- [Sunless Earth](https://what-if.xkcd.com/49/)

Read the article text itself, not search snippets or a tool's generated summary. Use a
page reader that returns the text, or fetch the HTML and extract its article body.
Recover clipped passages before writing. Complete references already in the current
context need no duplicate read. If a reference cannot be retrieved, state the gap and
try another available reading method; do not claim to have read it. A local repair or
review need only revisit the passages useful to that task.

Study the finished writing, not just the guidance below. Aim for the same ease of
following a difficult idea from its opening to its ending. The preserved local articles
in `src/reference-articles/` remain worked examples, but no longer set the prose voice.

## Build understanding step by step

Follow a question the reader has reason to ask. Each paragraph should grow from what
the previous one established. Use everyday words. Bring in a technical term when the
reader needs it, explaining the behaviour it names on the spot.

Stay with an example long enough to explain what happens, why it happens and what the
result lets us conclude. Put a mechanism into words before a formula, then explain what
the formula means. State assumptions where they enter the reasoning, and show where an
estimate or simplified model becomes unreliable.

Sentences can be short and still connect. Vary their length naturally; a word-count
target cannot tell you whether the reader can follow. Splitting a difficult sentence
does not supply a missing causal step.

Learn the explanatory craft without importing the essays' subjects, catchphrases,
absurd premises or xkcd in-jokes. Humour is welcome when it eases a difficult step or
marks a useful turn; it is not required. Their drawings do not prescribe our figure
style, and our prose must explain the subject without them.

## Stay Current's register

Use plain British English. Be warm towards the reader and fair to people making
different engineering choices. Explain why a choice makes sense in its conditions.
Avoid gotcha questions, invented objections and dismissive one-line verdicts.

Headings name the subject, using established, searchable terms where they fit. An
opening establishes a situation or question the reader can understand. End when the
explanation has earned its result, without adding a slogan or a moral.

Link to other people's writing on a useful phrase, and collect references under
Sources and further reading. Quote directly only when the wording itself matters.

## Say what you mean

Prefer literal language when it says what you mean. A metaphor that merely decorates a
setting, process or decision makes the reader translate it back. Watch for habits that
put the writer's performance ahead of the idea:

- An "it's not X, it's Y" reversal where a direct statement would do.
- Lists of three chosen for rhythm rather than meaning.
- Fragments used for drama, or a colon or dash held back for a reveal.
- Headings and openers that announce a lesson instead of naming its subject.

Keep each paragraph only if removing it would cost useful understanding. Length follows
the subject, including the help its difficult parts need. A necessary qualification
belongs with the reasoning it changes; repeated disclaimers do not make a claim more
accurate.

## Facts and examples

Engineering claims come from primary sources and verified work. Choose coherent
illustrative examples and make their assumptions clear. Measurements, execution results
and claims about what someone did need evidence; never invent them. Keep a material
unknown explicit in a draft and resolve it before calling the article complete. Ask the
owner only for facts that require their knowledge.

Ryan's descriptions can supply wording and facts when he has described the subject.
Stay Current is an engineering publication, so its teaching examples need not all come
from his personal experience. Distinguish the general principle from a worked product's
specific behaviour and guarantees.

## Repair without leaving patches

Treat a review finding as a problem the reader had, not wording to insert. Reword,
reorder, cut or move existing material; rethink the passage when repeated fixes do not
solve it. After substantive prose repairs, reread the whole article for repetition,
interrupting asides and new taglines. Keep the passages that already work.
