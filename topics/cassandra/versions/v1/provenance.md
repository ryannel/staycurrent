## Synthesis

- The stance, that Cassandra is for write volume with known queries and pays in flexibility, chores, and per-request consistency tuning, is the author's position argued from the sources above.
- The Facebook origin in 2008, the open-sourcing, and the 2010 Apache top-level date are stated from general knowledge; Wikipedia and the project history page were blocked.
- ScyllaDB's founding in 2015 by the KVM team, and the description of Seastar's per-core memory, network queue, I/O scheduler, and user-space cache, are from general knowledge of the project; the vendor's architecture pages were blocked.
- The read path description (data and digest requests, bloom filter, key cache, partition index, compression map, chunk cache, cell-level merge by timestamp) combines the storage engine, bloom filter, read repair, and cassandra.yaml sources with the author's understanding of the engine.
- The claim that logged batches write the batchlog to two other nodes is from the author's knowledge of the implementation; the guarantees page says the batch is replicated to another node.
- The working rule of 1 to 2 TB of data per Cassandra node, and the "days to rebuild a 4 TB node at 24 MiB/s" arithmetic, are the author's operational judgement and arithmetic on the documented streaming default.
- The per-node throughput figures (low tens of thousands of operations a second for a Cassandra node, several times that for ScyllaDB) are the author's experience, flagged as such in the text; neither project documents a figure.
- The capacity worked examples (messaging at 50 million a day, sensors at 50,000 a second) apply the documented partition formula and the author's assumptions for cell overhead, LZ4 ratio, and compaction headroom.
- The interview answers' recommendations (bucket sizes, salting with 16 shards, caching hot channels, adding one Cassandra node at a time, TWCS window choices, "plan on 2027 for Accord") are the author's judgement grounded in the mechanisms cited.
- Reaper and Medusa as the common external tools for repair scheduling and backups, and the "Postgres under a terabyte or two" threshold, are the author's view of common practice.
- The statement that ScyllaDB Cloud moved to AWS i8g instances in January 2026 was dropped from the article because only a page title could be seen.
