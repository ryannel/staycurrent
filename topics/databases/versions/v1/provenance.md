## Synthesis

- The stance itself: start on Postgres and leave only when a measured access pattern forces it, with the three exits named as multi-region writes, sub-millisecond reads, and billion-scale vector recall. This is the author's position, argued from the sources above rather than found in any one of them.
- "A database is a data structure you rent over a network" and the four-choices reading of every engine (storage layout, concurrency, replication and partitioning, query surface) are the article's organising model, drawn from the standard literature rather than a single citation.
- The mechanism explanations (heap versus clustered B-tree versus LSM versus column store, MVCC and the isolation anomalies, leader versus consensus versus leaderless replication, hash versus range partitioning) are textbook material, consistent with Designing Data-Intensive Applications, stated in the author's words.
- The claim that a single well-provisioned Postgres serves tens of thousands of transactions per second over terabytes is the author's working figure from experience, not a benchmark cited here.
- The threshold of roughly a hundred million vectors for a dedicated vector engine, and the statement that most "graph-shaped" domains are not traversal workloads, are the author's judgement, grounded in the vendor limits and the Stonebraker and Pavlo argument but not measured for this version.
- The two-billion-cell hard limit on a Cassandra partition is the figure in the project's data-modelling documentation, cited in the Cassandra deep dive's provenance; it was not fetched separately for this piece, and the 100 MB figure is the operational guidance above it.
- The description of Postgres full-text search as lacking BM25 ranking and offering only trigram similarity (`pg_trgm`) for fuzzy matching is written from the PostgreSQL documentation as the author knows it; the documentation site could not be fetched from the research environment for this pass.
- The replication diagram (leader-based, consensus, leaderless) is the author's drawing of the three write paths described in the text, not copied from any one source.
- The claim that a partitioned Postgres table with BRIN indexes handles a large share of time-series workloads before TimescaleDB earns its place is the author's experience, not measured here.
- The four worked cases (feed, ledger, metrics, retrieval) and the selection table are the author's application of the procedure to typical workloads; the engine recommendations follow from the documented limits cited above.
- Several vendor pages (AWS, Google Cloud, MongoDB, Redis, Snowflake documentation) could not be fetched directly from the research environment and were confirmed from their search-indexed content; each is cited at its primary URL and should be re-verified at the next run.
