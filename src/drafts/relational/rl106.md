A transaction across partitions must settle one outcome for records owned in different places. Each partition may already replicate its own state to survive a node failure. That replica agreement does not by itself make two partitions agree whether a purchase committed.

The distinction matters when failures separate the steps. One owner can be healthy while another is unreachable, or a coordinator can disappear after both prepared their changes. A safe protocol must retain enough durable evidence to recover one decision rather than let each participant guess.

Tenant t4's request r81 increments session s7's reserved count from 1 to 2 on partition P, and creates order o81 on partition Q. The order preserves its agreed total of 4,000 cents. We will inspect a conventional two-phase commit model, then identify how concrete distributed SQL products optimise or realise the same obligations differently.

## First agree within each partition

Imagine P has voting replicas P1, P2 and P3. A leader proposes a log entry, obtains the protocol's required agreement and establishes that entry in P's ordered history. Losing P1 need not lose the agreed change if the surviving replicas can continue the protocol safely.

Q has its own replica group. Its log can agree that o81 is provisional without deciding whether P's reservation and Q's order should both become committed. Consensus answers which history a replica group accepts. Atomic commit answers the outcome shared by all participants in this transaction.

Quorum agreement is also not the whole isolation guarantee. Readers and conflicting writers need a rule for provisional changes, dependency ordering and the chosen read snapshot. An atomic transaction can still run under an isolation level whose decisions admit anomalies. [Transactions and isolation](/review/relational/rl90/) supplies that separate prerequisite.

## Prepare reserves the ability to obey a later decision

In a conventional **two-phase commit** protocol, the coordinator first asks each participant to prepare. A participant checks its local part, makes the required provisional state durable and promises that it can later follow the shared decision. It replies prepared only after the protocol's durability requirements hold.

Prepared is not committed. P may retain an intent for reserved = 2 while ordinary committed state still says 1. Q may retain an intent for o81 while ordinary committed state still has no order. Conflicting operations can wait, help resolve the transaction or be rejected, according to the implementation.

A participant that cannot prepare reports failure. If the coordinator has not committed, it can decide abort, and participants discard the provisional changes. A participant cannot safely promise prepare, forget its state on restart and then act as though the transaction never existed.

The steps separate logical outcome from physical cleanup. Participants do not need to write their final representation in the same instant. They need a read/conflict protocol that interprets the same authoritative outcome while installation catches up.

## The decision must survive the coordinator process

Once every participant can commit, the coordinator records a durable commit decision before reporting completion under this protocol. It tells participants to commit. A lost notification can be resent; a returning participant looks up the decision rather than beginning a new independent transaction.

If the coordinator process crashes after that decision but before replying to the client, the transaction remains committed. The caller has an [unknown commit outcome](/review/relational/rl93/), not permission to repeat the business effect with another identity. If Q is temporarily unreachable after the decision, P cannot later decide to abort because Q's notification timed out.

If the coordinator disappears before a recoverable decision exists, prepared participants can be uncertain. Conventional two-phase commit can block until the decision authority recovers or an authorised recovery protocol resolves it. Replicating that authority improves its failure tolerance; it does not grant an arbitrary isolated participant the right to guess.

The model records a coordinator loss at exactly this boundary. P and Q retain their earlier committed values and intents. After recovery establishes commit, P applies first and Q later follows that same decision. Vary the history to recover abort before any commit decision; both must discard their intents. Attempting “P commits, Q aborts” violates the model's outcome rule.

## A healthy half does not guarantee transaction availability

Now make Q unavailable before it can prepare. P may have a full healthy replica quorum, plenty of capacity and the required row. It still cannot independently promise the complete purchase, because the order owner cannot participate.

Waiting may be bounded by a transaction deadline. If no commit decision has been made, the authorised coordinator can abort and release prepared work. If a commit decision already exists, a deadline does not reverse it. The application may receive uncertainty while the system continues finishing the committed transaction.

This is the availability cost of the cross-partition invariant. A larger number of participants raises the chance that one required path is slow or unavailable and increases routing and conflict work. Colocation can avoid that cost for common transactions. It is not merely a latency optimisation: it reduces the failure dependencies of the decision.

A global index can add a participant even when the visible SQL updates one table. The database must preserve that index's relationship with the row, so its physical placement belongs in the transaction's participant set.

## Concrete engines do not share one commit protocol

The two-phase model teaches obligations, not a claim that every distributed SQL engine sends these exact messages.

CockroachDB uses replicated write intents and transaction records. Its parallel-commit protocol can represent an implicit committed outcome through a STAGING record plus proof that its listed in-flight writes succeeded. A reader or recovery process can verify that condition; final intent resolution can follow asynchronously. It is misleading to describe every commit as waiting for one final COMMITTED flag before success.

YugabyteDB records provisional writes and transaction status using its own replicated tablet machinery. Spanner's read-write transactions use replicated participants and cross-participant commit coordination, together with its locking and timestamp rules. Their status, recovery and latency paths need implementation-specific evidence.

Standalone PostgreSQL supplies prepared transactions as a building block for an external coordinator, but does not automatically turn arbitrary connections to two servers into one distributed transaction. InnoDB XA and SQL Server distributed transaction facilities likewise require their supported coordinator and failure contract. Independently committing two ordinary connections is not an equivalent substitute. SQLite's local transaction interface does not provide a network-wide atomic purchase across independent services.

[The CockroachDB transaction investigation](/review/relational/rl124/) develops that concrete record/intent protocol. Keep documented product behaviour separate from the executable generic model here.

## Sometimes choose an explicit workflow

A workshop purchase may involve a payment service that cannot participate in the database protocol. An alternative is a durable workflow: hold a provisional reservation, authorise a payment, confirm the booking, then release or refund when a later step cannot complete.

That workflow exposes intermediate states. A refund is a new business action that can fail or arrive later; it is not a rollback that makes the earlier charge invisible. Other requests can observe the reservation or payment while the workflow is incomplete. Define deadlines, ownership, stable identities and reconciliation for every transition.

Use distributed atomicity when the invariant requires all-or-nothing visibility across participating owners and the system can operate its coordination contract. Use a workflow when the business accepts explicit intermediate states and compensation, or an external authority requires them. Calling a workflow a transaction does not remove its observable gaps.

For the capacity-2 session, a confirmed order without a reserved seat is usually unacceptable. Keeping those facts within one transaction is valuable. Sending its confirmation later is a suitable [outbox](/review/relational/rl94/) boundary because delivery can be retried without pretending the provider joined the purchase commit.

## Inspect the histories

Run [the model script](/review/relational/evidence/rl105-109-models.mjs) with Node. [RL106's recorded states](/review/relational/evidence/rl106-model-observed.json) show P, Q, their visible values, intents and coordinator decision at each step. Its assertions establish that the teaching history follows its declared rules. It neither starts a cluster nor proves durability under an engine crash.

When evaluating a product, demand a native exercise that loses the coordinator or relevant node at a documented commit boundary, then inspects the recovered operation by stable identity. Include a participant that is healthy while another is unavailable, and a lost client reply after the committed outcome. A healthy cross-range INSERT alone does not test those cases.

## Sources and further reading

- [Raft paper](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf), particularly log replication and membership change, defines one replica-agreement protocol. Its unit of agreement is distinct from this transaction-wide decision.
- [CockroachDB transaction layer](https://docs.cockroachlabs.com/docs/stable/architecture/transaction-layer) explains intents, transaction status recovery and parallel commits. Read its actual STAGING condition rather than substituting the generic two-phase picture.
- [YugabyteDB distributed transaction fundamentals](https://docs.yugabyte.com/stable/architecture/transactions/transactions-overview/) follows another implementation's transaction and status records.
- [Spanner life of reads and writes](https://cloud.google.com/spanner/docs/whitepapers/life-of-reads-and-writes) explains replicated participants and distributed transactions in that service.
- [PostgreSQL prepared transactions](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/sql-prepare-transaction.html) shows the responsibilities left to an external transaction manager.
