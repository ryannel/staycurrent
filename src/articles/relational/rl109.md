Adding a database node adds resources immediately, but useful data and authority may take time to reach it. **Rebalancing** moves replicas or ownership so the cluster can use those resources. During the move, foreground requests compete with copying, log catch-up and membership changes.

Removing a node has the inverse obligation: every required copy and authority must be safe elsewhere before its storage disappears. A node can be idle from the client's perspective while still holding replicas the cluster needs.

We will move workshop orders while writes continue, then change a replica group from A/B/C to B/C/D. The histories are executable teaching models. A concrete engine's movement, decommissioning and upgrade commands need its own native exercise; the CockroachDB implementation continuations are linked below.

## Copying bytes does not transfer authority

A live move begins from a consistent source snapshot and a change position. The snapshot supplies earlier state. A gap-free tail supplies every accepted change after that boundary, including updates and deletions. The source remains the authority until the protocol permits a change.

For this movement fixture, each order has an editable fulfilment estimate alongside its retained agreed total. We follow the estimate: t4's o81 starts at 4,000 cents, revision 1, and o82 at 6,000, revision 1.

The destination copies this snapshot at log position 100. While it copies, the source revises o81's estimate to 4,500 at revision 2, deletes o82 and inserts o83 with an estimate of 7,000. o81's agreed charge remains 4,000.

A count comparison would approve the wrong copy. Apply the ordered tail: 101 replaces o81, 102 deletes o82, 103 adds o83. The model compares identities and values in both directions after replay, rather than treating the equal count as completion.

The model also previews the membership and rate decisions developed below. First replay events 101–103 and compare every identity and value; then revisit its votes and catch-up controls after those sections.

Starting a change reader only after the bulk copy finishes would miss accepted work between its beginning and that reader. Replaying only INSERTs would miss the correction and deletion. Restartable progress must identify both the snapshot and the consumed change position.

A replay format also needs duplicate and ordering semantics. Reapplying an absolute revision condition can be safe; repeating an unguarded increment can double it. A deletion needs a retained record or tombstone until every required receiver can learn it. If the needed tail has already been discarded, a fresh snapshot can be necessary.

## Catch-up and membership are different stages

A newly copied replica may begin as a learner or non-voting member while it receives data. Counting an empty or badly lagging node as a healthy voter can reduce the actual redundancy behind an acknowledgement. The protocol must establish eligibility before depending on it.

For a fixed three-voter group A/B/C, a majority is two. We want B/C/D next. Simply switching names at different nodes can let A/B believe they form the old majority while C/D believe they form the new one. Those chosen majorities do not overlap, even though the complete memberships share B and C.

Raft's joint-consensus design illustrates one safe transition: an intermediate configuration requires a majority of both old and new memberships before the new configuration alone becomes authoritative.

This is one named membership protocol, not a claim that every engine exposes this intermediate state. The shared obligation is that changing who may decide must not allow incompatible authorities to form. Product implementations may use restricted single-member changes or optimisations with their own safety rules.

Leader or lease transfer is another operation. Copying D does not require moving the leader immediately; moving the leader does not imply moving all replicas. Which change the balancer makes affects foreground routing and latency differently.

## Planned removal must finish before storage disappears

A drain stops or redirects new client work and transfers responsibilities the implementation can move. Decommissioning removes the node from the intended topology and relocates its required replicas. A process shutdown alone does not establish either completion condition.

Before removal, verify that eligible destinations have room and satisfy placement constraints. A three-node cluster with three copies of every range cannot preserve three copies on distinct nodes after removing one without adding a destination or deliberately changing the replication contract. “Two live nodes remain” answers a temporary quorum question, not the final redundancy requirement.

Observe the actual completion evidence: no required replicas left on the retiring node, appropriate membership state, no under-replicated ranges and acceptable foreground service. Do not delete its volume because a decommission command merely started successfully.

A dead node changes the procedure. It cannot stream its own final data or drain clients. The surviving replicas must have enough authority to re-replicate from their accepted history. If quorum is lost, ordinary rebalancing cannot invent a trustworthy missing history. Forced recovery procedures may trade acknowledged data or safety properties and need the product's explicit contract.

## Budget movement beside normal work

A new node can raise latency before it improves capacity. Snapshot transfer consumes source reads, destination writes, network bandwidth, CPU and sometimes compaction or index work. Tail application competes with the continuing foreground log. Cold caches make newly routed requests more expensive.

Suppose a planned move copies 100 GB at an effective 100 MB/s. Ignoring every other cost, that is about 1,000 seconds, or 17 minutes. If foreground work generates a relevant 20 MB/s tail while the receiver applies 30 MB/s, it closes any accumulated tail at only 10 MB/s. These are invented constant-rate budgets; actual throughput changes with data shape and competing load.

Throttle and stage movement against request latency, backlog and disk runway. A lower copy rate can protect foreground service but extend the time spent under-replicated or carrying extra retained logs. A higher rate can finish sooner while saturating the source. Decide which budget is binding and observe both.

Do not assume equal bytes produce equal work. Moving a cold archival range can balance disk while leaving one hot session on the same leader. Splitting a range can spread independent keys; it does not split one incompatible counter decision. [Sharding and distributed SQL](/learn/databases/relational/partitioning-and-sharding/) follows that limit.

## Interruption needs durable movement state

Crash during the initial copy: the destination must discover whether it can resume its snapshot and tail, or discard the incomplete copy and restart. Crash after catch-up but before membership change: the source group's authority must still be discoverable. Crash during authority transfer: recovery must read the committed membership/ownership state, rather than assume whichever node started last wins.

After a cutover, the new authority can accept work that the old copy lacks. Reversing a routing entry to the old source is then data loss, not a rollback. Reversal needs a supported leader/member operation or a reconciled reverse move while preserving one authority.

For an application-shard move, every old connection must be subject to the ownership fence at effect acceptance. A stale routing cache carrying generation 1 must be rejected once generation 2 governs the shard. A generation stored only in application memory cannot fence a direct old writer. [Failover and fencing](/learn/databases/relational/failover/) teaches that boundary with a native process fence.

## Rolling versions are also a cluster change

A rolling upgrade keeps different binary versions alive temporarily. The supported version path and mixed-version protocol define what messages, storage formats and features they can share. “One node at a time” is insufficient if the product does not support that version combination.

Stage a supported binary change, observe the reduced capacity during each restart and verify that replicas recover before taking the next planned failure. Follow the product's recommended orchestration order; some operations should be grouped to avoid moving the same data repeatedly.

Feature activation or cluster-version finalisation can cross a different boundary from restarting binaries. Once a new on-disk format or cluster-wide feature becomes active, downgrading one binary may be unsupported. Keep rollback procedures and backups compatible with the actual stage, not just the package manager's old executable.

An upgrade rehearsal should include active transactions, lost client connections, replica catch-up and schema/background jobs. Do not combine a marginal disk condition, broad decommissioning and a version finalisation simply because each succeeds alone in an empty cluster.

## Inspect the model, then require native evidence

[The executable movement model](/learn/databases/relational/evidence/rl105-109-models.mjs) reproduces the write-during-copy, equal-count trap, replay and joint-majority calculation. Its `amount` field represents the editable estimate above. [RL109's states](/learn/databases/relational/evidence/rl109-model-observed.json) expose exact rows and membership assumptions. It tests a teaching history, not a native snapshot-transfer or consensus implementation.

[CockroachDB operations](/learn/databases/relational/cockroachdb/operations/) and [upgrading a CockroachDB cluster](/learn/databases/relational/cockroachdb/upgrades/) supply the implementation continuation. A useful native exercise adds a node with foreground identified writes continuing, observes replica movement, decommissions an eligible node and confirms every acknowledged request after completion. It also tests an interrupted move and the supported mixed-version/finalisation boundary. An unchanged row count or an increased live-node count is not completion evidence.

Citus shard rebalancing uses different data-copy and cutover machinery. Spanner manages many placement changes within its service. PostgreSQL physical replica replacement, MySQL group membership and SQL Server availability-group maintenance each have their own drain, membership and version contracts. SQLite core has no network cluster balancer. The common questions are history completeness, exclusive authority, sufficient surviving copies and the resource budget during change.
