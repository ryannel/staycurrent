## Ranked findings

1. **Low — Figure 6, “Many keys: jitter”: the 23 s chip partly covers P7 at 375 px.** This appears in the settled view in both light and dark. With the figure aligned near the viewport top, the visible P7 text occupied approximately x=51.08–70.39, y=93.12–112.62; the 23 s chip rectangle occupied x=63.24–108.45, y=99.37–121.98. The chip therefore intersects the right part of the key label by about 7 px horizontally and 13 px vertically. Screenshots visibly show the P7 label partly obscured. The text inside the chip does not itself intersect P7, which explains why a text-only geometry check need not detect this defect. Move the key label or the chip so the entire chip clears it. The time marker, other rows and comparison remain understandable. Desktop light has clear separation.

2. **Low — Figures 3 and 7: a few quantitative results need a small visible qualification.** In the unaided account, I could compare P7's stated 100 ms of work with the list's 3,000 ms, but could not tell why P7 had about five copies across a 20-server fleet. The prose supplies 250 requests/s × 20 ms and explains their distribution across servers. Likewise, the outage view shows 2,000 fills/s and 12% full cards from 20,000 requests/s without showing that joined callers contribute the extra two percentage points. The prose and long alternative explain this correctly. The source-saving choice and the outage's large fallback fraction were already understood, so these are not missing central reasoning. A short “arrival-rate estimate” qualification for the five copies and “including joined callers” for full cards would make those numbers easier to interpret without adding a derivation. The exact 17,700-load result may remain a reported simulation result.

3. **Low — Figure 7: “Queue deadline expires” names a stronger condition than the example shows.** The drawing has 8 ms left and a fill needs 20 ms. This correctly teaches refusal when insufficient useful time remains, not necessarily after the deadline has passed. A control such as “Too little time remains” would match the visible test. The numerical comparison and refusal-without-query outcome are clear; no wrong admission rule was inferred.

4. **Low — Figure 6, empty-cache view: “Every key needs a fill now” leaves the demand scope implicit.** I read this as the displayed requested keys being misses, but the drawing does not show those requests. In isolation the wording can sound like a direction to prefill the entire catalogue, whereas Figure 9 and the prose correctly prioritise demand and bounded useful warming. “Every requested key needs a fill” would remove that small ambiguity. I did not infer whole-catalogue prewarming in the unaided account.

5. **Low — Figures 6 and 10: some motion adds little to the settled explanation.** The jitter view mainly reveals three time chips. “Break the loop” reads as a useful static causal flow; I did not observe a meaningful changing state there comparable to the abandoned-query bar or retry multiplication. Keep the useful variants and final relationships, but these particular views could settle immediately or use less motion. This is optional simplification, not a reason to replace the sequence. The direct interventions, thresholds and feedback arrows do explanatory work even when static.

“Maps” in Figure 3 was undefined during the first reading, but the displayed callers converging on one query per server and the local-versus-fleet switch still taught the scope correctly. A more familiar label would be a small improvement; I do not classify the absence of map implementation detail as a missing mechanism. Similarly, Figure 8 requires reading the unit labels carefully because concurrency, starts per second, completions per second and connections differ. Those labels and the caption are correct, and that distinction is the lesson rather than a defect in itself.
