2. **Fresh checkout.** O14 existed in the query article, so I immediately need a reset. The first boundary paragraph explicitly says historical O12/O13 remain and O14 does not yet exist. The text correctly denies that a transaction discovers an omitted line.
3. **Checkout SQL.** RETURNING creates an explicit application branch: one row permits continuation; zero rows is successful SQL but unsuccessful reservation and requires rollback. The next block is conditional, on the same connection. I can map values to fields and see why order precedes line. Connection-pool guidance makes the shown boundary practically meaningful.
4. **Failure.** Independent fresh setup is stated both before and after the failed insert. The failure leaves the whole transaction failed; rollback discards the successful earlier writes too. Verification queries and table connect the outcome to concrete records. Savepoints are introduced as optional partial recovery, with an explanation of why they do not excuse abandoning a required line. Cancellation after commit is correctly a new operation.
5. **Concurrent fixed-value writes.** The example resets to one mug and assigns distinct purchases O14/O15 and distinct connections. Both read before either writes; A then writes/commits, B's blocked update resumes with stale computed zero. The non-negative constraint's inability to detect overselling follows directly. No need to imagine simultaneous CPU instructions.

Waiting itself is neither an acceptance nor rejection. The arithmetic-versus-fixed-value explanation gives the guarded update two distinct reasons to work: fresh subtraction and an enforced availability condition.
8. **Locking decision and schedule.** The partial-fulfilment example motivates a longer protected decision before returning to the one-mug comparison. Same-connection and lock-before-read requirements are explicit. The illustration highlights the difference between delaying an old decision and making a new protected one. In the model, committed counters and request panels have separate labels; the optional note makes the distinction explicit. The six-step schedule keeps A's pending stock change separate from committed availability until commit. A's rollback also has a useful outcome under the unsafe method: only B buys, which avoids presenting the method as wrong in every possible schedule.
9. **Snapshots.** Ordinary reads and locking reads are distinguished before the isolation comparison. The report/writer table identifies each event and its visible value. The two-connection instructions tell me where to pause rather than expecting both blocks to run straight through. 12. **Unknown commit and stable intent.** The two histories producing one missing reply are clearly stated. K14 survives request and attempt boundaries; it is not a replacement purchase. 15. **Closing review.** The final checklist is expressed as questions about concrete evidence and state. The reader can choose decline, whole-operation retry or recovery for the illustrated cases. Deeper isolation and full recovery branches have clear onward destinations.
