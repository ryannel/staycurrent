### Outboxes and database-backed jobs

This article makes a coherent next step from request recovery. Figures 1–2 show the two direct-send failures before offering an outbox. The shared transaction rectangle encloses order and intention, while the relay and receiver remain outside. It is particularly useful that “Pending · 29.00 · no payment” remains inside the order box and the intention says “Order received”: the drawing cannot be casually read as a payment confirmation.

The closest fixed comparison is **Sequence diagrams**, “Background workers and the transactional outbox”, figure 4. The reference shows request + job J9 committing, the browser's answer, the worker's later poll/claim, the provider's acceptance and the completion mark on distinct lifelines. The new article's figure 2 makes the atomic boundary more explicit, then figure 3 examines the failure interval that the reference passes to its companion. The change is justified by this article's promise: the shop's completion record can remain absent while the receiver count is already 1; after recovery the count becomes 2 without deduplication, and becomes 1 when the explicit ID-and-payload contract is selected. A newcomer can explain both what the outbox guarantees and why a local sent flag cannot guarantee one receiver effect.

The worker sequence also carries teaching rather than only terminology. Figure 4 distinguishes the lock before claim commit from the stored lease after it, and shows why an empty poll need not mean an empty queue. Figure 5 keeps `order-103-ack` fixed while A's generation 7 expires and B obtains generation 8; A's completion then matches zero rows. Figure 6 moves authority to the receiver and separately displays the equality case that fencing alone does not deduplicate. This resembles the reference storage drawings' deliberate separation of memory, table file, log file and application observation: distinct panels answer distinct questions, and labels explicitly state where the guarantee ends.

The body prepares the claim query by defining claim, lease and generation before using them. The lock-then-time-check completion example follows the lost-worker drawing where the reader now knows why a stale completion is dangerous. The late sections are denser than the opening, but their concrete decisions justify the space: an old acknowledgement redriven after receiver retention; a historical “order received” event versus a cancellable dispatch command; an exhausted job whose external effect may already have happened. “Four different meanings of stopping work” makes lease expiry, deadline, cancellation and deletion comparable without collapsing them into success or rollback. The optional schema opens cleanly and states the supplementary operational fields and permission requirement.
