## Figures first

- Figure 1 lets me explain the separate costs of finding four adjacent customer entries (two index pages) and locating their four scattered table rows. The root/leaf/table arrangement, `(page, slot)` label, counters and final highlighted rows make that relationship visible. I operated its four stages. It combines the fixed Indexes reference's entry-location and B-tree lessons in a smaller two-level example without losing the connection.
- Figure 2 makes three different costs directly comparable. At the final stage, customer-only reads/fetches four and sorts three; customer/time/ID reads/fetches two with no sort; time-first reads six entries but fetches only two. Numbered row labels and the identical result pair let the reader follow the same records in each ordering. I used all three choices. The lower-bound/LIMIT-2 caption is necessary and present. This is the strongest teaching figure: it shows why an index can avoid sorting yet still pass unwanted entries.
- Figure 3 separates four row fetches from four distinct pages in the mixed layout, then two pages in the grouped layout. Broadening to all three customers reaches all four pages again. The bars are explicitly row-fetch/page counts rather than timings; the caption excludes index and cache work. I can now explain why selectivity alone does not determine the route. I used both layouts and the narrow/all-customer contrast.
- Figure 4 distinguishes payload, visibility and subset membership. Key-only needs three row fetches; payload with confirmed visibility needs none while passing the same three entries; requiring visibility checks restores three visits; paid-only with checks needs one entry and one visit. The constant returned order 100012 is explicit. I used these meaningful comparisons. The deliberately simplified visibility switch is bounded by its caption rather than suggesting that this drawing is vacuum execution.
- Figure 5 ties the same pending-to-paid update to unchanged key values, changed leaf payload and new partial-index membership. It avoids promising zero key-index work. Its three arrows mean three consequences; on a phone they stack vertically. The adjacent prose explicitly calls them alternatives. No substantive misunderstanding seen, though a figure-only reader could initially read the stacked arrows as a chain; this is optional polish rather than an acceptance problem.
- The figures alone teach the article's central distinction: smaller traversal, fewer table visits and useful output order are different savings. The body must still supply expression matching, equality-column workload choice, mixed sort directions, joins/references and practical verification. That division is proportionate to a 15-minute access-path article.

## Body reading

The opening establishes one history request and the three savings before introducing mechanisms. Terms are given where used: entry/locator, B-tree/leaves/root, composite, selectivity, covering, partial. The customer/time/ID key is derived from actual SQL rather than simply prescribed. Paid-only history changes one requirement and forces a workload choice; it is not an unrelated indexing example.

The most useful passages are “Their union is not one newest-first list” under status position, the reverse-scan paragraph explaining that all varying directions reverse together, and the payload/partial contrast returning in measured results. They connect precise mechanisms to decisions as the fixed references do. The line “Finding the first of 80,000 entries quickly does not make reading the next 79,999 free” is plain and useful, not decorative shorthand.

The native repeated-search result is distinguished from Figure 2's hand traversal. Buffer accesses are also distinguished from distinct pages and storage operations. These qualifications prevent concrete mistaken inferences and sit with the comparisons they qualify.

Headings mostly name established terms or concrete questions: “Column order in a composite index”, “Covering indexes and index-only scans”, “Indexes for joins and foreign keys”. “Check that the proposed work disappeared” is less searchable than the reference's “Checking an index with EXPLAIN”, but it accurately states the article's decision test. I do not find an article-wide editorial failure or a substantive teaching stall.

Initial judgement: suitable beside the reference standard for this bounded purpose; no critical or medium reader finding identified. Optional polish: the final verification section could say “a PostgreSQL 18.6 server” rather than “the owned Linux PostgreSQL 18.6 server”; ownership is work-record language and adds no understanding. Figures and evidence remain clearly bounded even without that word.
