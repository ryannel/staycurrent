Sharding divides a database’s data into portions that can be owned and processed on different machines. One machine can handle Mina’s orders while another handles Tariq’s. A replica is another copy of a portion. Each shard can have replicas, so dividing the work and keeping surviving copies are separate decisions. The useful boundary comes from the requests. Keeping an order with its lines makes an order lookup local. Keeping a customer’s orders together also helps their history page. But Mina and Tariq buy the same products: putting their orders on separate machines does not give each machine a different last blue mug. This article follows an invented distribution of the collection’s shop. First we place customer work and shared product stock, then trace a catalogue-price change through three voting replicas. Finally we return to purchase 103 and ask what agreement within each shard leaves to a transaction spanning them. The drawings are models, not traces from a running cluster. Its boundary might be a range of customer identifiers, a set of hash values, or a portion of an engine’s ordered storage keys. Here we start with three customer shards, A, B and C, and a separate product shard P. The customer shards hold related customer, order, line and payment records. P holds the products shared by all customers. Figure 1 gives each shard three copies. Read across A’s row, then down machine 1’s column. The replicas add copies; the four shards divide ownership. Every machine stores a copy of every shard in this small model. A’s three cells are replicas of the same portion; they do not contain three disjoint thirds of A. Machine 1 holds copies of A, B, C and P. A machine is a place where work happens; a shard is a unit of data ownership. A real cluster usually has many more shards than machines, so ownership can be spread or moved in smaller pieces. Our small placement repeats every shard on every machine. It makes the distinction visible but saves no storage per machine. In a larger layout, placing different replica groups on different subsets of machines can spread stored data as well as active work. Replication still multiplies the bytes and copied write work for each portion. A local table partition is a different boundary. Twelve monthly partitions inside one PostgreSQL instance can help exclude months or retire old data without adding another machine’s capacity. Table partitioning and data lifecycle develops that choice. Here the question is who owns work across machines. A SQL connection’s destination need not own every row it requests. An entry node can locate the relevant owners and send work to them. A distributed SQL database provides routing, query execution and transaction machinery behind its SQL interface. With application-managed shards, more of those responsibilities may belong to the application. Similar SQL alone does not establish which cross-shard constraints or transactions a particular system supports. Choosing a shard key A shard key, also called a distribution key, supplies the information used to choose a row’s owner. Start with work the application repeatedly does together. An order page needs the order and its lines; a payment decision may need that order’s lines and recorded payments. Grouping them by order_id can keep that work together. Mina’s history page asks for several orders. Grouping by customer_id can keep all her order work together as well. In our model customer 1 selects A, customer 2 selects B and customer 3 selects C. Co-location and constraint scope Co-location means placing related data where its work can run together. A customer column on each table is only the beginning. Corresponding key values need compatible shard boundaries and placement, and the execution system needs enough routing information to use them. Independently hashing orders and lines by unrelated identifiers will not keep them together merely because they share an order number. Adding a routing column must also preserve what the keys mean. The shop’s order_id identifies one order globally. A local unique check on A and another on B can each accept 103. Changing the key to (customer_id, order_id) distinguishes (1, 103) from (2, 103); it does not enforce uniqueness of 103 alone. Those pairs are a counterexample, not two valid shop orders. Choose an actual enforcement mechanism for the original rule, or deliberately change the identity contract and every dependent reference. A globally enforced index may require remote work. A separate order authority must govern the relationship between identity and detail rows. An ID generator can reduce collisions, but does not by itself reject an explicit duplicate. The chosen engine’s constraint scope matters as much as where the rows fit. The same applies to references. A line routed under customer 1 must refer to the intended order, and carrying a customer column does not prove that relationship. Constraints or a complete write protocol must keep that association valid, including through imports, corrections and any movement between groups. Product stock remains shared. Copies of product names or catalogue prices can make a read join cheaper, under a declared freshness contract. They do not give every customer shard an independent allowance to sell P7. To call the purchase local, count every fact its rule must inspect or change, including constraints, indexes and shared stock. Range and hash distribution Range distribution keeps adjoining key values together. A query naming a range can identify the pieces that overlap it. Hash distribution first maps a key to a hash value and assigns that value to a bucket or hash range. Equal keys still choose the same group, while neighbouring original keys need not. Range locality belongs to the key that is actually distributed, not to any timestamp or identifier present somewhere in the row. Neither method guarantees balanced work. With a growing timestamp or sequence as the leading range key, new writes can concentrate at the high end. Hashing many distinct values can spread them, but a frequent value remains frequent. In the collection’s separate generated fixture, customer 1001 has exactly 84,000 of 120,000 generated orders. Hashing customer_id keeps that entire 70% in one customer group. Unequal stored size is data skew. Unequal request or write load is load skew. An old customer can own many quiet rows; a small product row can receive most purchase attempts. The generated count demonstrates the first kind, not a measured traffic distribution. Splitting one customer’s history by order can spread its storage and independent work, at the cost of sending that customer’s history query to several owners. Splitting requests for one stock row does not remove the need to arbitrate the same stock. A design that allocates separate inventory allowances is a change to the business protocol and must prevent the allowances exceeding available stock. Extra machines alone do not make that rule disappear. Queries across shards A request with a known shard key can go directly to the relevant owner. A request without it may need to fan out: ask several owners, then combine their results. Figure 2’s paid-order report computes a partial line value on each customer shard. A contributes Mina’s 39.50, B contributes Tariq’s 40.00 and C contributes no group. The entry point sorts the combined result. Pending order 103 is excluded; these are agreed line values, not payments received. The report does not need current catalogue prices, because agreed prices belong to the lines. A query asking for current product names would need those product records too. If their owner is remote, execution must fetch needed records, move rows for the join, or use a suitable maintained copy. Inspect where filtering, joining and aggregation happen: reducing rows before sending them can save network work. A secondary index or lookup directory can help a request that knows order 103 but lacks its customer key. The directory maps the order to its owner. That lookup has a location of its own, and writes and movement must keep it correct. Alternatively, an engine may fan out local index searches. “There is an index” does not tell you which routing path it supplies. Parallel requests can shorten elapsed time while increasing total work. A report waiting for every required owner also waits for the slowest one, and a failed participant can prevent a complete answer. More shards can mean more messages, partial results and combining work. A distributed plan should show the data and bytes moved as well as the rows returned. Fewer seconds and less work are different claims. One authoritative replicated history Dividing ownership leaves another question: how do copies of one shard agree what happened? Return to the separate catalogue experiment in which P7’s price changes from 20.00 to 22.00. Its stock stays at the post-purchase value 0; order 103 stays pending, with no payment and agreed lines totalling 29.00. What the acknowledgement promises The model assumes fixed membership, non-malicious replicas that can stop and recover, and storage that preserves acknowledged durable state. Its successful reply follows durable agreement by a majority and application at the leader. Losing one of three copies does not undo that agreement. Losing the surviving durable histories, storage dishonouring flushes, or restoring an earlier cluster falls outside that failure promise. The majority count is not the whole safety argument. Raft restricts elections using candidates’ logs and preserves the committed prefix across leaders. Terms distinguish leadership periods, and log-matching rules reject incompatible histories. Figure 4 deliberately uses a new entry from the current leader’s term: an entry from an older term cannot be declared committed merely by counting the copies that now contain it. A proposal whose reply never arrives may or may not have become committed. Resolve an uncertain application outcome using the request protocol developed in Retries and uncertain commit outcomes. After a failure, recovery must preserve that outcome even when not every participant has learnt it yet. A participant can have durable provisional work without being free to call it committed. Growing and changing the cluster An empty machine adds potential capacity. It relieves a bottleneck only after useful data or work reaches it. Moving a replica requires transferring a usable copy and catching it up with changes made during the transfer. Moving a leader can shift active work; moving stored replicas can change storage and survival. They are different changes. Ownership and voting membership must change without creating two authorities or discarding agreed history. A half-caught-up copy is not ready merely because its process is running. Copying and replay consume network, storage and processor capacity alongside foreground requests. Leave headroom for both planned movement and recovery from an unplanned loss. A cluster with a voter unavailable has already spent part of its survival margin; that missing redundancy is not spare capacity to consume casually during maintenance. A history page reading too many rows may need an access path. Expensive read traffic may fit qualified read replicas.
