# Relational data modelling

Factual neighbouring context, extracted from http://127.0.0.1:4606/review/relational-rebuild/modelling/. The fixed references lead writing and teaching.

A relational data model says what the database records and how those facts fit together. An order belongs to a customer. An order line records a quantity of one product at an agreed price. A payment contributes towards an order. Choosing the tables, keys and relationships gives each of those claims a place where it can be recorded, found and changed.

A design can store today’s rows and still be wrong. It may allow two current names for the same customer, require an order before a new product can exist, or mistake two lines containing the same product for a duplicate. The useful test is what happens when the shop changes its data: which fact changes, how many copies must change with it, and which associations survive.

The examples follow the small tea shop from the relational introduction. Mina Shah, customer 1, places order 103. It has one blue mug, P7, at 20.00 and two packets of green tea, P3, at 4.50 each: 29.00 in all. The figures are worked models of the shop’s rules, rather than recordings of a database run. Each figure starts from the shop’s starting data and shows the stated change. Together they develop one design and test the choices that would change its meaning.

What one row represents

Start a table by completing the sentence “One row records…”. For orders, one row records an order placed by one customer at one time. For order_items, one row records a numbered line of that order. For payments, one row records one payment towards an order. In prose we call an order_items row an order line.

The distinction matters as soon as an order has several lines and several payments. Order 101 has green tea and a teapot, worth 39.50 together. Payment 501 pays 30.00 by card; payment 502 pays the remaining 9.50 by gift card. Step through the figure and watch what happens when a row tries to represent a line and a payment together.

The four combinations are valid answers to “Which lines and payments belong to order 101?” They are a poor home for the underlying facts. Every payment is repeated for every line, and summing those repeated amounts doubles what was paid. More lines would produce more copies. Storing only two combinations instead would imply an allocation the shop never made.

Keep the order, its lines and its payments at their own levels. Both child tables refer to the order. If the shop later allocates part of a payment to a particular line, that allocation is another fact, with an amount and references to both. It should be recorded explicitly. Sharing an order does not establish that relationship.

Keys and identity

A key identifies a row across every state the shop permits, including rows it has not seen yet. Mina’s name is unique in the sample. That does not make it a customer key: two customers can have the same name, and one customer can change their name. Customer 1 remains the same customer through either event.

Candidate keys and surrogate keys

A superkey is a set of columns that identifies a row uniquely. A candidate key is a minimal superkey: remove any column and it no longer has that guarantee. Minimal means that none of its columns is unnecessary; it does not mean the shortest key among all the candidates. Choose one candidate as the primary key.

A natural key has meaning outside the database, such as a catalogue code that the shop promises to keep unique. Check the promise, its scope and what happens when that value changes. A surrogate key is an identifier introduced to name the row, such as a generated customer number. It lets references keep pointing to the customer when editable attributes change.

Adding a surrogate does not remove other uniqueness rules. If the shop gives each order line a separate line_id, it must still prevent two lines with the same (order_id, line_no). Otherwise two different IDs can both claim to be line 1 of order 103. A primary key on the ID alone cannot catch that disagreement. Keep the pair unique as well.

Composite keys

Line number 1 identifies a line only within an order. Orders 101, 102 and 103 can all have a line 1. The composite key (order_id, line_no) supplies the missing scope: line 1 of order 103 is one particular line.

Using (order_id, product_id) instead makes a different promise. It allows each product only once per order. Suppose the shop permits a second mug line, perhaps with a separately agreed price. Compare the two keys below.

Both rules can suit a shop. A cart that always merges identical products might want the unique order–product pair. A system that preserves distinct line occurrences needs a line key. Make that decision before choosing the key. Another identifier makes rows easier to reference; it does not decide which occurrences the business considers duplicates.

Relationships and cardinality

A relationship says which facts refer to which others. Its cardinality gives the allowed number on each side. Ask for both the minimum and the maximum. “A customer has many orders” leaves open whether a new customer may have none. “An order has one customer” leaves open whether that customer is required.

One-to-many and one-to-one relationships

For this shop, each order has exactly one customer, and each customer may have zero or many orders. Mina has two; Edda Berg, customer 3, has none. The reference therefore belongs in orders.customer_id. Repeating customer 1 on two orders records two different order–customer associations.

The words on each connection state participation. “0…many” includes customers with no orders and orders with no payments. The model does not declare that every order has a line.

The required foreign key on each order makes its customer mandatory and requires that customer to exist. It does not require every customer to have an order. The same distinction applies to order lines: each line has an order, but the line’s foreign key cannot require every order to have a line. If the shop allows empty drafts but requires at least one line before submission, that is a further rule about the order’s lifecycle.

To turn a reference into a one-to-one relationship, make the reference unique as well. For example, a product-detail table whose product_id is both its primary key and a foreign key permits at most one detail row per product. A product can still have no detail row. Requiring one on both sides needs an additional enforcement decision. Constraints develops how to make these rules hold when applications write concurrently.

Many-to-many relationships

An order can contain many products, and a product can appear in many orders. order_items represents that many-to-many relationship. Its rows do more than link IDs: each has a quantity, an agreed unit price and a line identity.

Quantity belongs to the line because neither the order alone nor the product alone tells you how many were bought. The agreed price belongs there for the same reason: P7 can be sold on different lines at different prices. A list of product IDs inside orders would leave those facts without a clear address. It would also make an individual product reference harder to check.

A linking table is not automatically keyed by its two foreign keys. A unique pair records membership, such as a product belonging to a tag. A line records an occurrence, which can repeat the same order–product pair. The shape of a many-to-many diagram does not settle that choice.

Functional dependencies

Keys tell us what identifies a whole row. A functional dependency says which columns determine another fact. Write customer_id → name and read it as “customer ID determines the customer’s current name”. Whenever two rows have the same customer ID, they must agree on that name.

This is a rule about every permitted state. It is not a pattern inferred from a few rows. The sample’s product names happen to be distinct, but that is no reason to declare name → product_id. The shop must be able to promise the distinction.

The first rule applies to all the lines of an order; the second applies across that customer’s orders too. The last requires the complete line key. Neither order_id nor product_id alone determines a line’s quantity.

Update, insertion and deletion anomalies

The name disagreement is an update anomaly: correcting one fact requires finding and changing several copies. Updating every copy in one transaction can make that particular correction consistent, but every writer must keep doing so.

The same design has an insertion anomaly. P9, the teapot, exists before anyone buys it. A table whose rows require an order and line number cannot record that product on its own. A fabricated order or an incomplete line is a symptom that the product needs an independent home.

There is also a deletion anomaly. In the starting data P9 appears only on order 101’s second line. If the flattened table is the only home for product facts, removing that last line also removes the only record of P9’s name and price. A product table lets the shop remove an association without accidentally removing the product.

Repeated values alone are not the problem. Customer 1 must appear on both orders to say who placed each. The anomaly is repeating the customer’s current name as though each order could decide it independently. Keep references where they express separate relationships; move the shared fact to the row its determinant identifies.

Normalisation

Normalisation uses dependencies to find and repair these kinds of redundancy. The normal forms name increasingly strong conditions on a table. They help explain why a repair works; they do not tell you which business facts to collect or require a separate table for every column.

Repeating groups and first normal form

Columns named product_1 and product_2 give an order an arbitrary capacity of two lines. A third line needs a schema change, and finding every order containing P7 requires looking in every slot. An encoded field such as "P7,P3" removes the column limit by making the application interpret a list inside the value.

The conventional first normal form, or 1NF, uses a single value from the column’s domain at each row–column position, without repeating groups of fields. For the shop’s lines, separate rows give every occurrence its own key, quantity and product reference. The application can then change one line or ask for all lines containing P7 using the same structure.

This does not mean every structured value must be broken into the smallest imaginable pieces. Whether a product description is one document or several facts depends on what must be addressed, checked and changed independently. The values article develops that boundary. Order lines already have independent identities and relationships, so hiding them in one value works against their meaning.

Partial dependencies and second normal form

After making line rows, suppose every row still stores placed_at. The key is (order_id, line_no), but the timestamp needs only order_id. This is a partial dependency: an order fact is repeated on every line even though the line number contributes nothing to it.

Second normal form, or 2NF, excludes partial dependencies of non-key attributes on a candidate key. Here those are the attributes that appear in no candidate key. Move placed_at, customer_id and status into orders; leave each line’s quantity and agreed price in order_items.

Adding a line_id does not repair the original dependency. If (order_id, line_no) still identifies lines, it remains a candidate key, and the order timestamp still depends on just part of it. Even if a design drops that uniqueness rule, the repeated fact remains: a normal-form label based on the new key would not make the copies safe.

Transitive dependencies and third normal form

The order now has its own table, but putting the customer’s current name there repeats it across orders. Follow the chain: order_id → customer_id → name. The order determines which customer placed it; that customer determines the current name. This is a transitive dependency.

For these shop tables, the third normal form, or 3NF, repair is to separate that non-key fact: store the current name in customers and retain only customer_id on the order. The same reasoning puts the product’s current name and catalogue price in products. It does not move the line’s agreed price there, because that is a different fact.

Boyce–Codd normal form, or BCNF, asks a stronger question: for every non-trivial functional dependency, is its determinant a superkey? “Non-trivial” excludes dependencies that merely repeat columns already on the left, such as (order_id, line_no) → order_id. A current customer name in the orders table fails: customer_id determines the name but does not identify one order.

3NF has a more permissive definition for attributes that participate in candidate keys, so it and BCNF differ in some designs with overlapping keys. Formal key derivation, those cases and decomposition procedures belong in the planned Normalisation and decomposition article. For this design, the practical question is already visible: does a fact depend on something that identifies several rows, forcing every one of those rows to carry the same answer?

Decomposing tables safely

Splitting a table is a decomposition. A useful split has to preserve the original information. Reconnecting its parts must recover the original associations without losing any or inventing others. A split that has that guarantee is lossless.

Separating customer names from orders passes the same test. An order holds one customer_id, and that key selects exactly one row in customers. Reconnecting them recovers the order and its customer’s current name without inventing a second customer for the order.

Preserving information is different from preserving dependencies. Suppose instead that we store (order_id, customer_id) in one table and (order_id, customer_name) in another. Both use the order key, so the split reconstructs order facts safely. Yet neither table by itself can check that every order for customer 1 uses the same current name. That check needs information from both.

A dependency-preserving decomposition lets the dependencies be enforced by rules on the individual resulting tables, without joining them to check the original rules. The customer table gives customer_id → name one clear home. Losslessness and dependency preservation answer different questions; a split may satisfy one and fail the other. The planned normalisation article develops formal tests and the cases where the stronger normal form trades against local enforcement.

Independent multivalued facts

Functional dependencies do not find every kind of repetition. Suppose P7 has the tags gift and ceramic, and can be bought from suppliers S1 and S2. These are independent lists: the tag does not say anything about which supplier supplies the mug.

If a table tries to record both lists together, it needs all four combinations. Leave one out and the absence appears to say something about that tag–supplier pair, even though the shop has no such rule.

P7 has two tags and two suppliers. In this scenario every supplier is independent of every tag. The four combinations contain only four membership facts: two tags and two suppliers.

No single tag or supplier is determined by P7, so the functional dependency product_id → tag would be false. The problem is that the whole set of tags is independent of the whole set of suppliers for that product. Adding a third supplier to the combined table requires two new rows, one per tag. A new tag would require one row per supplier.

Give each membership its own table: product_tags(product_id, tag) and product_suppliers(product_id, supplier_id). Each pair is unique. Now adding a supplier changes one membership, regardless of how many tags the product has. This is the kind of redundancy that multivalued dependencies and fourth normal form, or 4NF, describe; a table can pass BCNF and still have it.

The independence is essential. The supplier–product–location offer in Figure 4 asserted a particular three-way combination. Splitting it invented an offer. Here every tag–supplier combination is deliberately permitted, so the two independent sets preserve the intended information. Similar-looking three-column tables can need opposite decisions because they record different facts.

Two equal values may express different facts. Two different values may be conflicting copies of one fact. Before removing a value as redundant, ask exactly what it means and when it is supposed to change.

Totals, caches and denormalisation

Order 103’s line values are 1 × 20.00 = 20.00 and 2 × 4.50 = 9.00. Under this article’s rule, their sum determines its total: 29.00. There are no taxes, shipping charges or other adjustments in this example.

A stored orders.total calculated from those same lines is a second representation of the same fact. Change a quantity without refreshing the total and the two answers disagree. Deliberately storing such a copy to reduce read work is denormalisation. The saving must be worth the extra write work and the risk of disagreement.

| Useful saving | Measure the read work saved on the real workload against maintenance cost.

The answer changes if the shop records an independently agreed final charge, including an adjustment not recoverable from the lines. That amount is another business fact, rather than merely a cache of the line sum. Preserve the adjustment or the agreement that explains it. Similar column names are not enough to decide redundancy.

Begin with an unambiguous source of truth. Measure before storing copies for speed, and design their maintenance under concurrent changes. Indexes (planned) covers reducing read work; Transactions covers keeping related writes together.

Modelling variations in the data

The same questions apply when facts do not fit a uniform list of attributes. What does one row mean? Which identity do several kinds of fact share? Which relationships must remain visible and checkable?

Shared attributes and subtypes

Tea has an origin; teaware has a capacity. One products table could hold both columns, with a product type and unused fields for the attributes that do not apply. That is often manageable for a few stable variations. It needs rules distinguishing an inapplicable value from an unknown applicable one.

Another design keeps common product facts together and puts specialised facts in subtype tables. Here P3’s invented origin is Japan and P7’s capacity is 300 ml.

Optional subtype rows keep tea-only and teaware-only attributes with the products they describe. These references alone do not decide whether the subtypes overlap or cover every product.

Each subtype’s primary key is also a reference to products, so a tea detail row describes the same product that order lines reference. Adding the subtype does not create a second product identity.

Decide whether the subtypes are disjoint or overlapping: may a product have both kinds of detail? Decide whether they are total or partial: must every product have one, or can some have neither? Two foreign keys do not answer those questions. Choose between one table and several from the applicability and rules of the attributes, then arrange their enforcement. A separate table is useful when it expresses a coherent specialised fact, rather than merely moving a few empty cells elsewhere.

One parent per category can represent a tree, provided the shop also forbids cycles and decides how roots work. A foreign key accepts a parent that exists; it does not stop Mugs pointing to Blue mugs while Blue mugs points back to Mugs. Even excluding a category from being its own parent catches only the shortest cycle.

If Mugs can belong under both Teaware and Gifts, a single parent column is too restrictive. Use separate edges such as category_edges(child_id, parent_id), with the pair unique. The result permits shared ancestry and still needs a no-cycle rule if the categories must form a directed acyclic graph. Choosing traversal and reparenting methods, and comparing paths, closure tables and other representations, belongs in the planned Modelling hierarchies article.

Tables, JSON and entity-attribute-value models

A product’s presentation may include a small, variable description: tasting notes for tea, care instructions for a mug, or a display arrangement that changes with the catalogue. A JSON document can keep that material together when it is read and edited as one coherent document. Relational columns and documents can coexist.

The trade changes when the hidden fields carry established business rules. Putting an order’s products and quantities inside JSON makes the product references and line identity less directly available to ordinary foreign keys and uniqueness constraints. Putting capacity inside JSON may be reasonable for optional descriptive content, but less attractive once the shop must require a positive capacity for every teaware product and compare it consistently across the catalogue. JSON can be queried and validated; choosing it changes where those checks must be expressed.

An entity-attribute-value model, or EAV, goes further by storing rows such as (P7, 'capacity_ml', '300') in a generic attribute table. New attribute names need no new column. But the value column now mixes meanings: 300 ml and a country name do not have the same type or rules. Preventing two capacities, distinguishing unknown from inapplicable, checking units and connecting values to other entities all require additional design.

Use that flexibility when the attributes themselves are data the application must manage, such as a user-defined catalogue, and accept the need for attribute definitions, types and validation. For stable shared facts, named columns or coherent subtype tables usually make the rules easier to see. For independent relationships such as order lines and suppliers, retain explicit keys and references.

Assessing a schema

A schema review needs examples that exercise its distinctions. The starting rows are useful, but so are facts that have no associated rows yet, repeated occurrences and changes that affect several uses. Walk the proposed shop model through the operations below.

Where each change belongs

| The shop needs to… | The model must allow…

| Add Edda before she orders | One customers row, with no fabricated order.

| List a product before it sells | One products row, with no fabricated line.

| Put P7 on two separate lines | Two line numbers within the order, if the shop permits that; no accidental unique order–product restriction.

| Take a card and gift-card payment | Two payments rows referencing the same order, without duplicating its lines.

| Correct customer 1’s current name | One change in customers; existing orders still identify customer 1.

| Raise P7’s catalogue price | One change in products.price; the agreed 20.00 on order 103 stays put.

| Remove a line from an editable order | The association disappears; its product and customer remain independently represented.

Then read the relationships back as sentences. Every order identifies exactly one customer. Each line identifies exactly one order and product. An order may have several payments. Ask which rules are merely intended and which are actually declared. Positive quantities, existing references and unique line numbers can be explicit constraints. At least one line on submission, no category cycles and complete subtype coverage need further enforcement. A diagram that says “one” does not implement the promise.

Finally, ask the questions the application must answer. Can it find Mina’s orders, distinguish repeated mug lines, show what was agreed before a price change, and find unpaid orders such as 103? To calculate what remains due, it must total lines and payments at the order level before combining them; Figure 1 shows why combining their raw rows first repeats amounts. Detailed SQL belongs in Query results, but the relationships required for the answer must already exist.

A useful design lets a new fact arrive without inventing another one, lets a correction reach the fact’s authority, and lets an association disappear without taking unrelated facts with it. Keys and dependencies explain those properties. The change walkthrough tests whether the model has actually preserved them.
