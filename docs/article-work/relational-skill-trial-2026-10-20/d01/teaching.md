## Findings

### teaching-1 · required · a00, "Transactions and competing buyers", before Figure 3 and in the following two paragraphs

Problem: The article's central concurrency lesson depends on terms it never explains. "Isolation level" appears in section 1 ("kept apart according to a stated isolation level") and in the division-of-labour table, and then Figure 3 offers "Read Committed" and "Repeatable Read" as options without saying what either level lets a transaction see. "Snapshot" appears first in a Figure 3 step note ("a row that changed after his snapshot") and again in the InnoDB paragraph, with no definition. The row lock that makes Tariq's update wait, and that the guarded statement depends on ("rechecks the condition against the committed row once Mina's lock is released"), is introduced only through the step notes.
Effect: A newcomer can follow the outcome of each option but cannot predict it, and the paragraph that follows ("The isolation level is a contract, and its names do not mean the same thing everywhere"; InnoDB applies "the snapshot" to plain reads but not to updates) asks them to compare contracts they have not been given. The section's brief is to introduce isolation as a question the application must answer, and the reader cannot frame that question without knowing what a level governs.
Repair: Before Figure 3, add two or three sentences: an isolation level states what one transaction can see of others' changes while it runs; at Read Committed each statement sees what was committed when that statement began; at Repeatable Read the whole transaction reads from a snapshot taken at its first statement; and an update locks the row it changes until its transaction ends, so a second writer waits. a03 already has wording of this kind ("A steady view does not prove the rule") that can be shortened to fit.

### teaching-2 · required · a00, "Transactions and competing buyers", second paragraph

Problem: "That solves the first three rows of the table in section 1. It does not, by itself, solve the fourth: two complete, correct-looking transactions running at once." The table in section 1 has three rows. Its third row ("Reducing the stock … Nothing, unless Tariq read the stock while these writes were in progress") is the concurrency problem itself, which section 1 calls "the harder problem".
Effect: A reader who goes back to the table finds no fourth row, and is told that atomicity solved the very row that names the race. This blurs the distinction the section exists to teach, between all-or-nothing and protection against concurrent decisions, just before Figure 3 relies on it.
Repair: Say that a transaction solves the first two rows, the partial writes, and does not by itself solve the problem in the third row: two complete, correct-looking transactions running at once.

### teaching-3 · optional · a00, "Transactions and competing buyers", the durability and payment paragraph

Problem: One paragraph introduces the write-ahead log, `synchronous_commit`, replicas, idempotency keys and the outbox. The write-ahead log is not explained until "Running and recovering the database", and "replica" is defined only there ("A replica is a second server that continuously replays the primary's changes"), although section 4 already says "a replica may not yet have the change at all".
Effect: The reader meets five new mechanisms in a few sentences, two of them before their definitions, at the end of the article's densest section.
Repair: Gloss the write-ahead log at first use ("a record of each change that is flushed to storage before the commit is acknowledged") and either define a replica here in a clause or move that clause to section 8, where replicas are taught with Figure 4.

### teaching-4 · required · a03, "Retry an aborted transaction as a whole", the deadlock passage

Problem: The deadlock is told entirely in prose. Mina moves R8 from class 21 to 22 while Arun moves R7 from 22 to 21; each locks both class rows; Mina's transaction is aborted with 40P01; her next statement fails with 25P02; Arun's move continues, finds class 21 full "because Mina's release of her place there had been undone", and rolls back; Mina's retry commits. Then, with ordered locking, "Arun's transaction waited for row 21, then both moves committed". The starting state is not restated: Figure 1's rows do not include R8, so the reader must infer that the booking from "Put the condition in the change" committed and that class 21 now has 0 places and class 22 has 2.
Effect: The reader has to hold two classes' counters, two bookings, two lock holders, a victim and a retry in their head across six events, and then a second schedule for the ordered version. This is the changing state that every other race in the article shows as a step table, and the passage is where the article teaches why a known abort means reconsidering the whole transaction.
Repair: Add a third scenario to the schedule figure, or a short step table in the same form as Figure 2, for the opposite-order moves: the starting state (class 21: 0 places, R8; class 22: 2 places, R7), each lock request, the wait cycle, the 40P01, the 25P02, Arun's empty result and Mina's retry, with committed state per step. Add the ascending-order run as a second option of the same figure so the reader can compare the cycle with the queue. The replies are already observed and checked by `verify.mjs`.

### teaching-5 · optional · a03, opening of "Start with the rule that must stay true" and the close of the article (the coordinator's question on the class-booking example)

Problem: a03 replaces the introduction's shop with classes, reservations and instructors. Judged against the commission, the switch helps more than it costs. The class example gives the three shapes of rule the article teaches, a stored count, a uniqueness rule and a rule over several rows, in one small schema where each arises naturally. The staffing rule in particular produces write skew without contrivance, which the shop would have needed an invented rule to do. The example also matches the data-design article (a01, a booking service for classes) and both children: a18 explicitly "keep[s] the class reservations" and removes the counter, and a19 follows "class 21's last place" and its two instructors, so a reader taking the depth route never changes example. The costs fall in three places, each cheap to remove:
1. The reader arrives with the mug race fresh from a00, and a03 re-teaches the same lost update with a new cast and new tables instead of building on it. Nothing says that class 21's last place is the mug race in another form.
2. The cast is reshuffled. Tariq was the rival buyer in a00; here he is an instructor, and a new customer, Arun, is the rival. The children differ again: a18 and a19 make Mina and Tariq the rival bookers, and a19 makes Mina and Tariq the instructors.
3. The hand-off out of a03 lands on an argument a03 does not make. The next working-route article, a04, opens with "The transaction article supplies the argument that the order, items and guarded stock reservation are correct under concurrency" and then refers to "No stock reserved" as a business result. The trial a03 never returns to the order and stock.
Effect: A newcomer spends a figure learning new tables and names to relearn a race they have already seen. They may wonder why Tariq is now teaching, and they reach a04 told that an argument about the shop has been made when they saw it made about classes. Each is a small stall, not a misconception.
Repair: Keep the class example. In the first paragraph, map it onto the introduction: `remaining` plays the part of the mug's stock and a reservation plays the part of an order line, so Figure 2's first example is the mug race with the booking row made visible; then go straight to what is new. Keep Tariq as the rival customer, as the children do, and give the instructors other names. In "Check the argument with competing sessions", add one short paragraph that applies the finished argument to the shop's order: guarded stock update, unique request identifier, payment recorded as intent. That gives a04 the argument it assumes.

### teaching-6 · optional · a03, Figure 1 and the capacity invariant

Problem: The introduction taught that "storing each fact once is the point, not a tidiness preference". a03 then stores the same fact twice, a `remaining` counter beside the reservation rows, and its first invariant exists only to keep the two in agreement. Figure 1 notes that they "describe the same fact twice, so they must agree", but nothing says why the design keeps the counter.
Effect: An attentive newcomer's first question is "why not count the bookings?", and the article leaves it unanswered until the reader happens to open a18, which removes the counter.
Repair: Add one sentence: the counter gives every booking a single row to put its condition and its lock on, which is what makes the one-statement protection possible; counting the reservations instead is the harder case that Designing changes that coordinate with locks takes up.

### teaching-7 · optional · a03, "Coordinate decisions that span several rows" and the closing argument

Problem: The criteria for choosing among the protections for a rule over several rows are all present but spread across three sections. The locking protocols work "only if every writer follows them"; Serializable protects only "between transactions that both run at Serializable"; its false positives make "the retry rate rise with contention"; and each choice changes what the losing request sees. The closing argument then picks "the staffing change locks the class row first" without saying why it was preferred to Serializable or to locking the counted rows.
Effect: The reader can repeat the article's choice but would have to rebuild the comparison to decide differently for their own rule, and does not see what would change the choice. That decision is the one the article exists for.
Repair: After Figure 3, or in the closing section, add a compact table: rule shape, protection, the guarantee it relies on, what every writer must do, and what the loser sees and the application does. Give the reason for the coordination row in the closing argument, for example that every staffing path is in code the team controls and a wait is easier to handle than a retry. Name what would change the choice, for example a writer the team cannot make take the lock, which points to Serializable.

### teaching-8 · optional · a03, "Check the argument with competing sessions", step 4

Problem: The procedure asks the reader to test "an insert of a row the decision did not see". The article states this risk in prose ("It does nothing about a row that did not exist yet") but never shows it. None of its three examples can produce the case: the counter gives the capacity rule a single row, and a new instructor can only help the staffing rule.
Effect: The reader is told to test a failure they have not seen and may not know how to construct, at the point where the article hands them the method.
Repair: Either point step 4 at Designing changes that coordinate with locks for the worked insert race, or add one concrete sentence: if capacity were checked by counting reservations with `FOR UPDATE`, a second booking's new reservation row would not be among the locked rows, so neither transaction would wait.
