**The likely quality blind spots**

The reference headings mostly identify durable concepts: “Lifelines and messages”, “B-tree”, “Range scans”, “Buffer pool”, “Dirty pages”. The trial repeatedly announces conclusions using abstractions: “The engine chooses work behind the statement”, “Copies and partitions change where a request goes”, “Choose an arrangement from the work it must support”.

Those phrases can pass “say what the section covers” while still feeling unlike the references. The rubric establishes intelligibility, not successful imitation. The assignments further emphasise “concrete reader effects rather than preferred wording”; the severity guidance treats a heading as an example of optional low polish. My inference is that a reviewer could notice the stylistic discrepancy yet regard it as outside substantive review.

The same distinction applies beyond headings. The references repeatedly let an observable event carry an explanation: a search reads particular pages; a worker claims J9; P7’s new price reaches one location before another. The trial’s coverage obligations plausibly encourage compressed distinctions and duty lists. The first editorial finding explicitly calls one paragraph an inserted coverage checklist. That is evidence of the symptom, not proof that the brief caused it.

Partitioned checks also leave a gap between “legible and correct” and “teaches the first idea”. The first practitioner and visual reviews found nothing; the coordinator supplied the missing first-table teaching finding. More reviewers using the same acceptance model do not necessarily close that gap.

For quality judgement, add one short instruction to editorial: judge whether the finished article could sit beside the references as the same author’s work; report a substantial failure even when every sentence is understandable and no local error stops the argument. Ground that judgement in specific passages. This preserves reference-led evaluation without manufacturing another style checklist.
