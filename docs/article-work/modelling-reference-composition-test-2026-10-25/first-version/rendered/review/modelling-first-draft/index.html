Relational data modelling

Deep diveAI-generated

A data model decides which facts a database can keep, how it tells them apart and which facts can change independently. A table might keep an order, one line of that order or one payment. All three belong to the same purchase, but putting them in the same row changes what the database can say.

The test is what happens next. Can the shop add a product before anyone buys it? Can Mina buy the same product on two lines? Can she pay once by card and once by gift card? When the blue mug’s price rises, does yesterday’s order keep the price she agreed?

This article designs the structure that answers those questions. It follows the shop from the relational introduction: customers place orders for tea and tea things, numbered order lines record what they buy, and payments settle orders. The examples are small invented states of that shop, not measurements of a running database. They start with facts we want to preserve, then change one choice at a time.

What one row represents

Start by finishing the sentence “One row says that…”. In orders, one row says that order 101 was placed by customer 1 at a particular time and has a particular status. In order_items, one row says that line 1 of order 101 is one pack of green tea at an agreed unit price of 4.50. In payments, one row says that payment 501 put 30.00 towards order 101 by card.

These are three different levels of fact, sometimes called the grain of a table. The order’s date applies to every line. A line’s quantity applies to that line alone. A payment’s amount applies to the payment, which can cover more than one line.

Order 101 has two lines and two payments: 30.00 by card and 9.50 by gift card. Figure 1 puts the lines beside the payments. Follow payment 501 down the result.

Payment 501 appears twice, once for each line. So does payment 502. Adding the displayed payment amounts would give 79.00, twice the 39.50 that was paid. Nothing about those four rows tells us that a particular payment belongs to a particular line. It only tells us that both belong to order 101.

Keep a row for each line and a row for each payment, each referring to the order. If the shop later needs to allocate payments to lines, that is another fact: how much of payment 501 was allocated to line 1, for example. It needs its own relationship, rather than an accidental pairing in a report.

A page can display all these facts together. That does not make the page’s shape a suitable storage model. Name the fact a row asserts before choosing the columns to put in it.

Keys and identity

A key identifies which instance of that fact a row describes. The database must be able to distinguish two customers called Mina Shah, two orders placed at the same instant and two lines containing the same product. A sample in which names and times happen to be unique does not establish that they are keys.

For the shop, customer_id identifies a customer and order_id identifies an order. Mina can correct her name without becoming a different customer. Changing the name in customer 1’s row should leave orders 101 and 103 referring to customer 1.

Candidate keys and surrogate keys

A candidate key is a set of columns that uniquely identifies a row under the business rules, with no unnecessary column. A set that identifies a row but may contain unnecessary columns is a superkey. If product_id identifies a product, then (product_id, name) is a superkey too, but the name adds nothing to identification. It is not a candidate key.

There can be several candidate keys. If a shop assigns each product a required, unique catalogue code as well as an internal product ID, either can identify it. Choose one as the primary key and preserve the uniqueness of the other. Making the product ID the primary key must not allow two products to acquire the same supposedly unique catalogue code.

A natural key uses a value already meaningful to the business, such as that catalogue code. A surrogate key is introduced to identify the row, such as an internal number with no further business meaning. A natural key is useful when its meaning, uniqueness and stability are dependable. Check what happens if a code is reassigned, a supplier changes its numbering or two businesses merge. A surrogate gives references an identity independent of those changes, but does not settle the business rules on its own.

Composite keys

A composite key has more than one column. The shop numbers lines within each order, so line_no = 1 can appear in orders 101, 102 and 103. The complete key is (order_id, line_no). Neither part identifies a line alone.

Choosing (order_id, product_id) instead would say something different: a product may occur at most once in an order. Suppose a customer needs two lines for the blue mug, with different agreed prices. The shop’s line key permits that; the order-and-product key would refuse it.

And still decide whether repeated products are allowed. Two different line IDs would otherwise accept either a deliberate second line or an accidental duplicate.

Relationships and cardinality

A relationship says how facts are associated. Its cardinality says how many associations are allowed. Read it in both directions, asking for a minimum as well as a maximum.

Each order in this shop identifies exactly one customer. One customer can have zero or many orders: Mina has orders 101 and 103, Tariq has 102, and Edda has none. “Many orders” does not mean “must have an order”. The minimum is zero on the customer’s side and one on the order’s side.

One-to-many and one-to-one relationships

For this one-to-many relationship, the reference goes in the many side: each orders row carries a customer_id. The customer needs no growing list of order IDs. Figure 2 reads the same kind of rule for orders, lines and products.

The required customer reference gives each order a customer; it does not give every customer an order. Similarly, the order reference gives each line an order, but it does not require every order to have a line. If submitted orders must have at least one line, record that rule separately. A diagram should say whether it describes all stored orders, including a basket being assembled, or only submitted ones.

A one-to-one relationship adds an upper limit of one in the other direction. Suppose the shop introduces a separate order_receipts table, with at most one receipt per order. Make its order reference unique, or make order_id its primary key as well as its reference. Two receipt rows then cannot identify the same order. An order without a receipt is still possible. “At most one” and “exactly one” are different requirements.

These choices describe the intended rules. Constraints covers how required references, uniqueness and rules involving several rows enforce them.

Many-to-many relationships

An order can contain many products, and a product can appear in many orders. A table between them records those associations. In this shop it is order_items, and its rows carry more than two references: each has a line number, quantity and agreed unit price.

The quantity does not belong in products, because different lines buy different quantities. It does not belong in orders, because one order can buy a mug and two packs of tea. It describes the line’s participation in this order.

Some relationship tables represent a unique pair. A product-tag table might allow P7 to have the tag “gift” only once, with (product_id, tag) as its key. Other relationships represent repeatable occurrences, as the shop’s numbered lines do. Before choosing the key, ask whether a second occurrence of the same pair is a duplicate or another fact worth keeping.

Relationships involving three things

Two-way links cannot always express a complete relationship. Suppose a supplier offers a particular product to a particular shop location. An offer from S1 for P7 at North says nothing about whether S1 offers P7 at South.

Figure 3 has three actual offers. Try to rebuild them from only supplier-product, supplier-location and product-location pairs.

S1 offers P7 somewhere. S1 serves South with something. South can receive P7 from someone. Together those statements still do not say that S1 offers P7 to South. Keeping only the three pairs discards which three things belonged together.

Keep offers(supplier_id, product_id, location_id), with one row per offered combination. Under this example’s rule, each combination appears once, so all three columns form its key. An offered price would belong to that combination too. If offers can recur over time, their identity needs another distinction; a date or offer ID would need a stated rule, just as a line ID did.

Functional dependencies

The shop’s tables work because each fact has a home. A functional dependency makes that reasoning precise: X → Y, read “X determines Y”, says that rows agreeing on X must agree on Y in every permitted state of the table.

In a flattened table with one row per line, order_id → placed_at: all lines of order 103 must show the same placement time. customer_id → customer_name: every occurrence of customer 1 must show the same current name. But order_id does not determine product_id, because one order has several products.

“Determines” is a rule about meaning, not a claim that values never change. Customer 1 can change their name; the rule requires all copies of the current name to agree afterwards. Nor does a dependency follow from three convenient rows. If every order in a sample contains a different product, that does not make the order a product’s identity. Ask what the shop permits tomorrow.

Update, insertion and deletion anomalies

Suppose all the facts above live only in the flattened order-line table. Correcting Mina’s current name means finding every one of her lines, across every order, and changing each copy. Miss one and the database gives two current names for customer 1. This is an update anomaly: one business change needs several coordinated edits to copies of the same fact.

A new product exposes an insertion anomaly. The shop knows the product’s ID, name and price before any order includes it. A table whose key is an order and a line has no honest row in which to put that product. Inventing an order, or leaving part of its identity missing, is a sign that the product needs a home of its own.

Removing the last line for P9 exposes a deletion anomaly. If P9’s name and current price exist only on that line, removing a purchase also removes the catalogue fact. The shop may still sell P9. Separating products makes removing a line and removing a product different decisions.

Repeated IDs are useful here. Customer 1 appearing in two orders records two associations. P3 appearing in two lines records two purchases. The anomaly is repeating a fact such as customer 1’s current name in places that can disagree, rather than repeating the reference that tells us whose order it is.

Normalisation

Normalisation uses dependencies to find these misplaced facts and separate them without losing their associations. The normal forms name particular tests. Their value is the fault they expose: an arbitrary limit on items, an order date repeated per line or a customer name repeated per order.

Repeating groups and first normal form

An orders table with product_1 and product_2 has room for exactly two products. A third needs another column, with another quantity and price column beside it. A question such as “Which orders bought P3?” has to check every product position.

Putting "P7,P3" into one text field removes the fixed limit but hides the items inside an encoding. Which quantity belongs to which product? How do we change line 2’s agreed price or check that every listed product exists? The application has to parse and maintain a structure the table no longer exposes.

Separate order lines give each item an address, (order_id, line_no), and its own quantity, price and product reference. This is the practical reason for the first-normal-form treatment of repeating groups: represent the individual values the relationship needs to work with. “One value” depends on the chosen domain and operations; it does not mean splitting every string into its smallest pieces. The values article develops that choice.

Partial dependencies and second normal form

The flattened table now has one row per line, but still carries the order date. Its line key is (order_id, line_no). The date depends on order_id alone, only part of that key. Quantity needs the whole key: order 103 has quantity 1 on line 1 and quantity 2 on line 2.

This is a partial dependency. Second normal form rules out a non-key attribute, one belonging to no candidate key, depending on only part of a candidate key. Move the order-level facts into orders, identified by order_id, and let every line refer to its order. Step from the first to the second stage of Figure 4 and watch the dates.

The date now appears once for each order, however many lines it has. Adding a surrogate line_id to the flattened table would not have moved that fact. While (order_id, line_no) remains a candidate key, the partial dependency remains too. Looking only at the chosen primary key can hide the problem.

Transitive dependencies and third normal form

The second stage still puts Mina’s current name in both order rows. The dependency follows a chain: order_id → customer_id → customer_name. The name depends on the order through another fact, the customer’s identity. This is a transitive dependency.

Move the name to customers, with one row for customer 1. An order keeps its customer reference. The third stage also separates current product names from lines. The last stage makes a hypothetical correction from Mina to Meena: one customer row changes, while both orders keep the same reference.

For these tables, that removes the non-key facts depending on other non-key facts, the problem third normal form diagnoses. This description is sufficient for the shop’s straightforward keys; the precise definition also deals with attributes that belong to overlapping candidate keys.

Boyce–Codd normal form, or BCNF, gives a stronger test: every non-trivial determinant must be a superkey. A determinant is the left side of a dependency; “non-trivial” means it determines something not already in that left side. In the flattened table, product_id determines the product name but does not identify a line. In the separated products table it identifies the whole row, so that dependency fits.

These labels do not decide whether the shop needs receipts, whether a product can appear twice in an order or whether a name means its current value or its value at purchase. Establish those meanings first. A formally tidy table can still describe the wrong business.

Decomposing tables safely

Splitting a table is a decomposition. The split is useful only if we can recover exactly the original associations. Consider a smaller flattened table with (order_id, line_no, placed_at, product_id, quantity, unit_price). Split it into orders holding (order_id, placed_at) and lines holding the remaining columns, including order_id.

To reconstruct line 1 of order 103, take that line and find its order by 103. There is exactly one order row, because order_id is its key. That row supplies the date, and the same happens for every other line. No line gains a second date or a different order. This is a lossless split: joining the projections of the original table recovers the original facts.

The full shop can also store products with no purchases and orders with no lines. A view made by joining only existing lines cannot display those independent rows. That is a limit of that view, not evidence that their separate tables lost information.

Now try splitting an association on a value that is not its key. Figure 5 starts with just two lines, then removes the order ID from the product side. Watch what happens when line 1 is matched back.

Both orders match both product rows. We still have every original row’s values, but no longer know which values belonged together. The reconstruction invents two purchases. “Lossy” can mean losing an association in this way, even when the immediate symptom is extra rows.

There is a second question besides reconstruction: can the dependencies still be checked using rules on the separate tables? A split is dependency-preserving when those local dependency checks together imply all the original functional dependencies. A lossless split need not have that property.

Take (order_id, customer_id, customer_name), with the rules that an order has one customer and a customer has one current name. Splitting it into (order_id, customer_id) and (customer_id, customer_name) keeps both rules in tables where they can be checked. Splitting it into (order_id, customer_id) and (order_id, customer_name) can also reconstruct the original rows by order ID. But each table could pass its own key checks while orders 101 and 103 give customer 1 different names. Checking agreement by customer now needs information from both tables.

The planned Normalisation and decomposition article will own the formal tests, candidate-key derivation and decomposition procedures, including the cases where BCNF and dependency preservation compete. Here, the working questions are whether the split preserves the associations and where each original rule can still be checked.

Independent multivalued facts

Functional dependencies do not explain every kind of repetition. Suppose P7 has two tags, “gift” and “ceramic”, and two suppliers, S1 and S2. The tags classify the product independently of who supplies it. Neither supplier owns one of the tags.

If one table keeps (product_id, tag, supplier_id), representing both complete sets requires every tag-supplier combination. Figure 6 shows the four rows for P7.

Adding a third tag requires a row for S1 and another for S2. Removing a supplier means taking care not to lose the last surviving occurrence of a tag. A product with tags but no suppliers has no complete row in this combined table.

No rule says P7 has only one tag or only one supplier, so neither is functionally determined as a single value by the product. The repetition comes from combining two independent sets. Multivalued dependencies describe this kind of rule; fourth normal form addresses the resulting redundancy. Keep product_tags(product_id, tag) and product_suppliers(product_id, supplier_id) separately, each with its pair as a key.

The independence is essential. Figure 3’s offers describe which supplier offers which product at which location. Those combinations are the fact, so breaking them into independent pairs invented an offer. Before separating sets, ask whether every cross-combination really is intended. Formal multivalued and join-dependency analysis belongs in the planned normalisation article.

Historical facts and derived data

Two values can look like copies while expressing different facts. Conversely, two differently named columns can still be copies that must agree. Decide which question each value answers before trying to remove repetition.

Current price and agreed price

Order 103 buys one P7 mug at 20.00 and two P3 tea packs at 4.50 each. Later, the shop raises P7’s catalogue price to 22.00. Figure 7 changes the current product price and leaves the line alone.

products.price answers “What does the shop charge for P7 now?” The line’s unit_price answers “What price was agreed for this line?” Product P7 can appear on different lines at different agreed prices, so product_id → unit_price is not a rule of order lines.

Replacing the agreed price with a lookup of the current price would make order 103 total 31.00 after the increase. Its actual lines still total 29.00. The agreed price has its own meaning, even if it originally came from the catalogue.

The same question applies to names and addresses. A customer’s current name and the name printed on an issued document may need different homes. This article’s customer-name dependency explicitly means the current name. Effective dates, historical versions and corrections need further rules, developed in data history and lifecycle.

Totals, caches and denormalisation

The line total is quantity × unit_price, and the order total is the sum of its line totals. In this example there are no taxes, delivery charges or additional discounts. Order 103 is therefore 1 × 20.00 + 2 × 4.50 = 29.00.

If orders.total stores that same calculation, changing a line creates work in two places. A quantity change that leaves the stored total alone gives two answers to the same question. Unlike the agreed unit price, this total is derived from facts already present.

Storing it may still be useful. A busy order list might otherwise sum many lines repeatedly. This is denormalisation: deliberately storing a derived or repeated representation to save work. Start from evidence that a required read is costly, then define the copy’s contract.

Transactions owns that coordination; indexes and performance supply ways to investigate the read cost. A stored total earns its maintenance cost only when it saves useful work.

Modelling variations in the data

The shop’s basic model has a few regular kinds of row. New requirements can introduce attributes that apply only sometimes, relationships back into the same table or details whose shape varies. The same starting question still works: what fact must be independently identified, changed or checked?

Shared attributes and subtypes

Tea has an origin and a brewing temperature; teaware has a material and a capacity. One products table could hold all four columns, with a product type saying which apply. That keeps a product together, but a mug’s missing brewing temperature then means “does not apply”, while a tea’s missing brewing temperature might mean “not yet known”. It also becomes possible to fill in tea attributes on a mug unless rules prevent it.

A second design keeps common facts in products, then gives each subtype a table keyed by the same product ID.

P3 can have a tea-details row and P7 a teaware-details row. Order lines still refer to products, so a purchase needs no separate tea and teaware references. The cost is that reading the complete description of a tea needs both its common and its tea-specific facts.

Decide whether the subtypes may overlap and whether every product must belong to one. The references alone allow a product with neither details row or with both. If the shop means “exactly one of tea or teaware”, that is a rule to enforce; the drawing and the table names do not enforce it. A gift set might also expose that the original two-type choice was too narrow. Choose from the attributes and rules the shop needs, rather than creating a subtype table for every label in the catalogue.

Recursive relationships

A category can refer to another category as its parent. In categories(category_id, name, parent_id), parent_id references categories.category_id. Tea could be below Drinks, with Green tea below Tea. The root’s parent is absent.

One parent column permits at most one parent per category. If Green tea can sit under both Tea and Gifts, use a separate category_edges(parent_id, child_id) table, with the pair as its key. A category can then have several incoming parent relationships.

Neither representation prevents cycles merely by pointing at existing rows. Tea can point to Green tea while Green tea points back to Tea, and both references still find valid categories. A tree also needs rules about cycles and roots; a hierarchy with several parents still needs cycle prevention if it is meant to represent ancestry.

The planned Modelling hierarchies article will compare adjacency lists, edge tables, paths, closure tables and nested sets, and develop traversal and reparenting procedures. Here the first choice is whether a category has one parent or can share ancestry. That choice changes the relationships the schema must hold.

Tables, JSON and entity-attribute-value models

A small product-description document might hold optional tasting notes, a maker’s care instructions or extra display details that vary across products. A JSON column can keep that document with its product. It can be a reasonable boundary when the document is read and changed as a unit, and its internal fields are not the main relationships the shop must enforce.

That reasoning does not extend automatically to hiding all order lines inside an order document. The shop needs to identify line 2, reference its product, check its quantity and total its agreed price. Those established facts already have a useful table structure. Moving them into JSON transfers those jobs to document-specific rules and queries; valid JSON alone says little about valid shop data. PostgreSQL’s document-design guidance likewise recommends a predictable structure even where flexibility is wanted.

An entity-attribute-value model, or EAV, moves the column name into data. A row might say (P7, 'capacity_ml', '300') in a generic (product_id, attribute, value) table. Another row might say (P3, 'origin', 'Japan'). Adding an attribute no longer needs a new column.

The generic shape leaves questions the original columns answered. Is '300' a number? Is 'large' allowed for capacity? Can one product have two capacity rows? Does capacity apply to tea? A key on product and attribute can settle the duplicate question, but typed values, allowed attributes and applicability need more machinery. An attribute catalogue and separate typed value storage may supply it, at the cost of building part of a schema inside the data.

EAV can serve a system whose purpose is to let users define new attributes. JSON can serve a bounded, variable document. Ordinary columns and relationship tables keep established, shared rules visible. Choose the flexibility the product needs and account for the checks it moves elsewhere; neither a column per possible detail nor one generic bucket is a universal design.

Assessing a schema

A schema is ready to assess when each table has a clear row meaning, each key preserves a required distinction and each relationship has stated limits in both directions. Then walk the operations the application must perform. Use examples that push against the rules, not just the rows already in the database.

Required reads test the associations too. “Show Mina’s orders” should follow customer 1 to orders 101 and 103. “What is due on order 103?” needs its two lines and its payments, of which there are none, giving 29.00 due in this example. Keep the line sum and payment sum separate before comparing them; Figure 1 showed what combining both sets row by row does. Queries develops the SQL for those results.

Finally, separate a rule the structure can express from the mechanism that makes it hold. The composite key distinguishes lines. A foreign key prevents a non-empty reference from naming a missing order. A required reference prevents an order without a customer. But “a submitted order has at least one line”, “this hierarchy has no cycles” and “the stored total agrees with all its lines” need further enforcement. Write those rules beside the model so that they survive the move from design to implementation.

The useful outcome is a change whose meaning is clear: a new product creates a product, a new line creates a purchase occurrence, a corrected current name changes a customer, and a catalogue price rise leaves the earlier agreement intact. When one operation unexpectedly changes another fact, return to the row meaning, key and dependency that made them share a home.
