# The buffer pool: brief and topic tree, 17 October 2026

Companion to `relational/storage.astro` ("Storage and durability"). Page:
`src/pages/learn/databases/relational/buffer-pool.astro`, registered in
`src/lib/relational-collection.ts` (storage group, status `draft`). Draft only;
nothing here authorises publication.

## Reader and job

A working backend engineer on PostgreSQL, MySQL (InnoDB) or SQLite who has read, or
will read, Storage and durability. They have to choose `shared_buffers`,
`innodb_buffer_pool_size` or `cache_size`; explain a slow period after a restart or
failover; and tell whether the cache is doing its job. They know what a page, a hit,
a read and an eviction are from Storage's "A cache of pages in memory" section; that
section stops at two paragraphs on the operating system's cache and the 25% / 80%
starting points.

Useful stopping point: the reader can predict what the cache will keep under their
workload (including after a large report), pick a starting size and defend it against
the machine's other memory consumers, plan for a cold cache, and read the cache's own
hit and miss counts without being misled by them.

Central question: what decides which pages stay in memory, and how much memory should
that cache get?

House approach: general mechanism under general names; PostgreSQL 18 as the labelled
worked example; InnoDB (MySQL 8.4) and SQLite where they change the reader's prediction.
Running example: the shop from Storage (products, an index on product_id), plus a
`page_views` table that a nightly report scans.

## Topic tree (derived before drafting)

Each node: what the reader needs, then the depth decision.

1. **What the pool holds** — the reader needs: pages of tables *and* indexes share one
   pool; a query's page requests include index root and branch pages; the idea of a
   *working set* (pages a workload touches repeatedly over a period), which every later
   section uses. Depth: short section (3 paragraphs). Do not re-teach hit/read/evict;
   link to Storage `#buffers`.
   - 1a. Index pages and the B-tree shape (root/branch pages tiny and always hot).
     Depth: a paragraph with arithmetic; feeds the hit-rate section.
   - 1b. InnoDB: the table is the clustered index, so "table pages" are index leaves;
     undo pages also live in the pool. Depth: one sentence.
   - 1c. Other memory PostgreSQL keeps outside shared buffers (temp-table local buffers,
     work_mem). Depth: deferred to sizing; one clause.
2. **Two caches: the pool and the operating system's** — needs: whether a miss reaches
   the drive depends on the engine's I/O path; double buffering; what follows for sizing
   and for restart. Depth: section, ~4 paragraphs.
   - 2a. PostgreSQL buffered I/O: a miss may be an OS-cache hit; a page can sit in both
     caches; why the docs say 25% and "unlikely more than 40%". Observation: reads at a
     few microseconds per page from the OS cache. Depth: substantial paragraph.
   - 2b. InnoDB `O_DIRECT` (8.4 default on Unix where supported): a miss is a device read,
     no double copy, hence 80%. Depth: paragraph.
   - 2c. SQLite: small per-connection cache; the OS cache is the shared cache between
     connections and processes; `mmap_size` reads through the OS cache directly. Depth:
     paragraph.
   - 2d. Prediction: a database process restart leaves the OS cache warm (PostgreSQL,
     SQLite) but not InnoDB's pool; a host restart empties both. Depth: sentence here,
     developed in 6.
3. **Which page leaves: eviction and scan resistance** — needs: why plain LRU fails under
   a one-off scan; each engine's defence and its limits; how to see what the pool keeps.
   Depth: the largest section, h3s and the interactive figure (eviction under a scan
   with a policy switch and scan length). Local observations for PostgreSQL and SQLite.
   - 3a. LRU and sequential flooding (a scan larger than the cache evicts everything and
     hits nothing). SQLite's default page cache is LRU; observation: a 771-page table in
     a 2 MB cache gets zero hits on the second scan. Depth: subsection.
   - 3b. PostgreSQL clock sweep with usage counts (max 5; new page starts at 1; hand
     decrements). Protection proportional to how often a page is used, bounded by scan
     length. Observation: a 3,000-page index range scan left the hot set cached with its
     counts drained from 5 to 1; a second one evicted it. Depth: subsection.
   - 3c. PostgreSQL buffer rings: sequential scans of tables larger than a quarter of
     shared buffers, VACUUM (`vacuum_buffer_usage_limit`, 2 MB) and COPY/CTAS (16 MB) reuse
     a small ring. Observation: 96 buffers after a 14,815-page parallel scan; hot set
     intact. Limits: index scans, bitmap scans, bulk UPDATE/DELETE that dirty ring pages.
     Depth: subsection.
   - 3d. InnoDB midpoint insertion: old sublist 3/8, `innodb_old_blocks_time` 1000 ms; why
     a scan's pages stay old; limits (time window; pages revisited after a second;
     `innodb_old_blocks_time = 0`). Documented, not run (no MySQL locally). Depth:
     subsection.
   - 3e. Seeing it: `pg_buffercache` / `pg_buffercache_usage_counts()`;
     InnoDB `PAGES_MADE_YOUNG` / `PAGES_NOT_MADE_YOUNG`, "youngs/s"; SQLite cache
     hit/miss. Depth: short subsection; the catalogue belongs to Watching storage.
   - Out: InnoDB read-ahead tuning (one clause), adaptive hash index, pool instances'
     effect on eviction.
4. **Dirty pages in the pool** — needs: the pool also holds changes waiting to be written;
   evicting a dirty page needs a write (and the log first), so a read can wait on a write;
   a larger pool holds more dirty pages, so checkpoints write more (PostgreSQL's advice to
   raise `max_wal_size` with `shared_buffers`); InnoDB `Innodb_buffer_pool_wait_free`.
   Depth: short section (3 paragraphs), linking Storage `#dirty` and `#checkpoints`.
   Do not re-teach dirty pages or checkpoints.
5. **Choosing a size** — needs: working set versus database size; the knee; the memory
   budget; per-engine starting points; signs the pool is too small. Depth: section with h3s
   and one arithmetic table (a modelled workload; labelled as arithmetic).
   - 5a. Working set and the knee: model of 100 GB database, 99% of page requests on
     8 GB. Depth: table + paragraph.
   - 5b. What else needs the memory: PostgreSQL `work_mem` per operation per session,
     `maintenance_work_mem`, connections, OS cache; InnoDB ~10% control-structure
     overhead plus per-connection buffers; SQLite cache × connections; swapping is worse
     than a smaller pool. Depth: paragraph + worked budget for a 64 GB host.
   - 5c. Starting points: PostgreSQL 25% (rarely > 40%), InnoDB up to 80% on a dedicated
     server, `innodb_dedicated_server` formula; SQLite raise `cache_size` per connection.
     Depth: paragraph.
   - 5d. Too small: steady misses per second that the drive feels; PostgreSQL
     `pg_stat_io` evictions; InnoDB `wait_free`. Depth: paragraph; full metrics in
     Watching storage.
   - Out: huge pages (one sentence), NUMA.
6. **A cold pool after a restart or failover** — needs: what warming costs (arithmetic:
   pages × read rate); restart vs host reboot; saving and reloading the pool's page
   list; replicas and failover. Depth: section, three h3s.
   - 6a. Cost of a cold cache: arithmetic with stated assumptions. Depth: paragraph.
   - 6b. InnoDB dump/load (defaults on, 25%, `ib_buffer_pool`, background,
     `Innodb_buffer_pool_load_status`, abort); PostgreSQL `pg_prewarm` autoprewarm
     (preload library, `autoprewarm.blocks`, 300 s, two workers) and manual `pg_prewarm`.
     Observation: 444 table pages restored after restart; without it, the first query
     read all 444. Depth: subsection.
   - 6c. Failover: the standby's pool holds what replay and standby queries touched, not
     the primary's read set; warm candidates; send reads to standbys; prewarm on promote.
     Inference from mechanism, labelled as such. Depth: subsection.
7. **Reading the hit rate** — needs: how each engine counts; deltas over an interval, not
   since reset; the ratio is per page request (inner B-tree pages inflate it); 99% vs
   99.9% is ten times the reads; PostgreSQL "reads" may be OS-cache hits (use I/O timing);
   ring-buffer scans; better questions (misses per second, evictions, waits). Depth:
   section, ~5 paragraphs plus a small per-engine formula table. Metric catalogue →
   Watching storage (sibling draft; link only if the page exists at build).
8. **Changing the size while running** — PostgreSQL restart only; InnoDB online in chunks
   (`chunk_size × instances` rounding; waits for active transactions; new ones wait while
   it runs; `Innodb_buffer_pool_resize_status`); SQLite any time per connection, lost on
   reopen. Depth: short section.

Deliberately out: InnoDB change buffer, adaptive hash index, compression's unzip LRU,
NUMA, huge-page setup, PostgreSQL 18 asynchronous I/O beyond its effect on ring size,
kernel page-cache tuning (`vm.*`), managed-service sizing presets.

## Figure decision

One stepped figure: eight pool slots, four hot shop pages, a report scanning pages
once. Policy switch (LRU; clock sweep; clock sweep with a ring; midpoint insertion) and
scan length (short, long). Stats: hot pages still cached, shop misses afterwards. It
carries the comparison prose cannot: the same scan, four policies, the slot-by-slot
outcome. It is a model; the real observations are in prose and labelled.

Other carriers: a modelled sizing table (5a), a per-engine hit-rate table (7), SQL
snippets for `pg_buffercache` and `pg_prewarm`.
