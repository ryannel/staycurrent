# Body prose review

15 October 2026. Scope: the current body prose in Indexes, How a query runs and Storage and durability, including their seven figures. This is a new editorial reading after the user's rejection of the headings. The earlier “ready as a draft” verdict is not evidence that this writing feels natural. No article files were edited.

I reread the current source, the Stay Current style guidance already loaded in this conversation, and the original passages from [Gears](https://ciechanow.ski/gears/) (“Disc” and “Transmission”) and [How to teach technical concepts with cartoons](https://jvns.ca/teach-tech-with-cartoons/) (“personify the characters” and “show a scene”). The original opening of [Load Balancing](https://samwho.dev/load-balancing/) was read during the first review. Their useful shared quality here is that the object, action and consequence stay in view as the explanation develops. The recommendation is not to reproduce any author's mannerisms.

**Verdict: revise the prose, then review the revised passages in their surrounding sections.** The technical explanations are mostly understandable and often strong. That does not settle voice. Too many paragraphs briefly leave their subject to announce a lesson, describe the investigation, defend its scope or advertise a source. The result is repetitive and more formal than the underlying ideas require. A sentence can be easy to decode and still be an awkward thing for a person to say.

## Recurring problems

1. **Announcing the reasoning instead of doing it.** “The useful question,” “that distinction,” “this observation,” and “the useful lesson” repeatedly stand between the example and its consequence. These expressions are not inherently wrong. Their frequency and placement make several paragraphs feel like summaries of an explanation rather than the explanation itself.
2. **A useful metaphor carries too much of the text.** Routes, runs and work are valuable in the index introduction. Later, conditions are “doing navigation,” candidates receive “the work [they] will have to live with,” and a “route” repeatedly substitutes for a scan, plan or ordered set of entries. Specific actions would be clearer and less mannered.
3. **Evidence is reported in the voice of a review record.** “Abbreviated observation,” “those fields establish temporary-file work,” and “the concrete engine here” describe authorial procedure. The reader usually needs what PostgreSQL did, what a number means and which conditions affect the result. Keep provenance, but give it a useful place.
4. **Paragraphs repeatedly finish with a moral and a documentation description.** General reminders are sometimes essential. Repeatedly moving from the example to a maxim and then “the documentation explains…” gives sections an identical, stop-start rhythm. A relevant link can often sit on the factual phrase it supports; a further-reading sentence earns its space when it names a new question the reader can investigate.
5. **Short sentences sometimes produce staged emphasis rather than easy reading.** The articles often have good short sentences, but “The index definition had not changed. The table's state had” sounds arranged for effect. Connecting those statements can clarify the cause while keeping the pace natural.

## Passage-level repairs

Line numbers refer to the source at this review; the quoted words are the stable locator. These examples are repair directions, not mandatory replacement templates. Preserve the surrounding technical conditions and links.

### Indexes

**Opening, “The useful question is what route this particular query needs.”** The preceding paragraph has already introduced a direct route to data. This sentence announces the question and repeats the metaphor before giving the useful fact: an index on customer ID still leaves filtering and sorting. Start with that fact. For example: “An index on customer ID can find this customer's orders, but the database may still have to check their dates and sort them.” Continue into how a different key arrangement helps. Keep the promise to examine maintenance costs, but let it follow the choice rather than sound like a syllabus.

**Opening, “The concrete engine here is PostgreSQL 18.”** This is project/report language. “We'll use PostgreSQL 18 and its usual ordered index, a B-tree” is more direct. Retain the separation between general key-order reasoning and PostgreSQL's table storage and visibility implementation. The distinction matters; “concrete engine” does not help express it.

**B-tree section, “We pay for navigating to the relevant part, then for reading its matching entries.”** The tree is well explained just before this, but “we pay for” and “relevant part” blur the actual operations. “The database first descends the tree, then reads the matching leaf entries” keeps the reader with the mechanism. The range-query continuation can then show the scan continuing along the leaves. The existing sentence about more orders enlarging the bottom before adding another level is worth retaining.

**Filtering, line 108: “An index being used does not mean every query condition is doing navigation.”** This is an awkward abstract subject and an overstretched metaphor. The preceding example has the exact objects: customer, date and status. Prefer “The index uses customer and date to find candidates; PostgreSQL checks status after fetching them.” Then introduce `Index Cond` and `Filter`, preserving the qualification that an index condition on a later key may reject entries without reducing the scanned interval. This keeps the useful technical distinction while avoiding the slogan.

**Filtering, line 111: “It earns its space only for queries whose conditions let the planner establish that all needed rows are in that subset. A parameter whose value is unknown when a reusable plan is made can prevent that proof.”** The technical point is sound but presented as a compressed logical proof, after several other caveats. Let the pending-order example carry it: the planner must know that the query asks only for pending orders before it can use an index containing only pending orders. Then explain that an unknown parameter may prevent it from knowing that. This is a case where a little more concrete reasoning would read more naturally than a shorter abstract sentence.

**Covering indexes, lines 120–122: “That distinction is visible in our experiment”; “To reach the next state in your practice session”; “The index definition had not changed. The table's state had.”** These phrases surround a clear result with a description of the teaching sequence. Prefer “Before vacuuming, the plan reported `Heap Fetches: 10`. Run…” and, after the command, explain how the new visibility marks allowed those ten visits to be skipped. Keep autocommit, eligible pages, the unchanged index, and the limit that busy pages may not keep those marks. The point is to make the causal sentence do the work of the staged contrast.

**Storage and maintenance, line 131: “Changing an indexed value requires maintaining its new position; including a frequently changed amount also creates upkeep even though that amount does not guide searches.”** “Maintaining its new position” is hard to picture, and “creates upkeep” is an abstract substitute for updating the entry. Use the two actual cases: changing a key requires index entries for the new key value; changing an included total also requires maintaining the index, though total does not control its order. Preserve PostgreSQL's version-maintenance qualifications rather than imply a simple in-place move.

**Testing, lines 149–153: “Finally, give the candidate the work it will have to live with”; “Those mutations are deliberately visible”; “a specific possible saving…for a specific storage and update cost.”** The first sentence is a conspicuous flourish; the second describes the author's presentation choice, and the final repetition of “specific” feels manufactured. Begin with “Now insert, update and delete some orders with the covering index in place.” Say what the script checks and what this temporary-table experiment cannot measure. End with the actual decision: whether avoiding table fetches is worth the extra space and maintenance for this workload. Retain the detailed representative-workload guidance; that is useful closure, not expendable caveat material.

### How a query runs

**Opening, line 77: “That change gives us a way to investigate execution”; “deliberately give the planner a question its initial statistics misunderstand.”** The first sentence is an abstract restart after a strong concrete opening. The second personifies the planner imprecisely: the statistics give a poor estimate; they do not misunderstand a question. Continue directly with how PostgreSQL handles the customer report and whole-shop report, then promise a case where its row estimate is wrong. Keep the parent prerequisite and the new investigation clear.

**Nested-loop joins, line 99: “Each returned line carries the matching order's contribution into the join.”** This sounds explanatory but adds little identifiable information after the trace through 42/1, 42/2 and 42/3. “Contribution” is especially vague before the aggregation section. Remove it, or say simply that the join produces one result row for each matching order line. The concrete trace preceding it should remain.

**Hash joins, line 116: “The naturally chosen plan changes in two places”; “Here is the abbreviated observation.”** This reads like verification notes. Use “PostgreSQL chose a hash join and a hash aggregate for this query” and introduce the shortened plan. Preserve the fact that default planner settings produced this plan and that another installation may choose differently; that qualification can remain in the plan introduction or evidence note.

**Hash joins, lines 120 and 123: “This observation explains a choice; it is not a claim…”; “The useful question is how many rows…”** These endings make the reader leave the actual lookup and assess the lesson's scope twice. Keep the necessary performance limit plainly: these plans show the work PostgreSQL chose for the generated data, and the example has not compared elapsed times. Then connect the row counts to the next decision instead of giving another general maxim. The hash-table construction cost and the 10,000 separate searches are already a concrete explanation.

**Planner estimates, lines 150–151: “No orders changed and no search index was added. We improved information used to choose a plan”; “The useful lesson is to identify what information is missing.”** These sound like a conclusion repeated immediately after its demonstration. A more connected passage would say that the estimate rose from one to ten after PostgreSQL learned the customer/region dependency, while the bitmap plan stayed the same. Then distinguish stale statistics from an unrecorded relationship directly. Retain the same-table equality scope; do not turn the example into a claim that dependency statistics solve all estimation errors.

**Memory, lines 158–161: “temporarily give a detailed line listing a small allowance”; “Those fields establish temporary-file work”; “A spill is a reason to inspect the amount and shape of input, not an instruction…”** “Detailed line listing” and “small allowance” make the setup more obscure than its SQL. Name the action: lower `work_mem` for this transaction and sort all 30,000 order lines. After the result, say that PostgreSQL wrote temporary files, retaining the observed size, the build dependence and the PGlite limitation. The final paragraph can ask whether the application needs every order line or just totals; that concrete choice explains why raising the setting is not the only response. Keep the warning about several operations and concurrent queries each consuming memory.

### Storage and durability

**Opening, lines 23–24: “These jobs meet at a deceptively small event”; “Understanding that distinction explains both…”** The commit question is strong enough without an announcement of its significance. Move directly from tables and stored bytes to the application's successful commit reply. After explaining that the table page may still be unwritten, state what WAL preserves. Keep crash recovery versus lost-storage recovery in the article, but avoid loading the opening with a description of all the lessons it will deliver.

**Rows and pages, line 35: “This gives physical arrangement a consequence the query does not reveal.”** This is the clearest instance of the style problem: the sentence is comprehensible but sounds like a summary of an explanation. Delete it and start with the next sentence, “Fetching one price can bring neighbouring rows into memory too.” That sentence already does the explanatory job. Keep the following reuse and scattered-row examples.

**Updates, line 45: “PostgreSQL uses a log to make a different promise.”** The promise metaphor delays saying what differs. The paragraph has already set up the cost of writing all changed pages before replying. End by introducing logging enough information to recover those changes while writing the pages later. The next section can then explain how WAL enforces that order. Keep the distinction between memory changes and durable-write ordering.

**WAL, line 50: “The essential ordering rule is between durable writes.”** The next clause is the rule. Lead with that rule: before PostgreSQL writes a changed data page to storage, it flushes the WAL describing those changes. Then explicitly retain that the in-memory page can change earlier. This is simpler without weakening the technical statement. The page's log position can follow as the mechanism enforcing it.

**After a crash, line 59: “PostgreSQL's transaction guarantee concerns the complete committed change, rather than whether traces of unfinished work occupy storage.”** This abstract wrap-up follows a good concrete comparison of 20.00 and 22.00. It repeats the result in harder language. Keep the comparison and use a shorter direct connection if needed: an unfinished row version can remain in the file without becoming visible as a committed change. The important distinction must remain, but need not be restated as a definition of the guarantee.

**Vacuum, line 73: “Cleanup is part of sustaining this storage design, even when the application's number of current rows stays steady.”** The sentence is a report-level summary after the reader has already seen the old and new versions. “A table can therefore need regular cleanup even if its row count stays the same” says the consequence directly. The descriptions of autovacuum, page pruning and free space may stay; do not imply vacuum returns all space to the filesystem.

**Durability, lines 81–83: “The same COMMIT spelling does not turn their contents into a durable record”; “Now destroy the storage”; “demand different evidence”; “preserving and testing a way back.”** The first is contrived, the second a jarring command, and the ending becomes abstract at the point of decision. Say directly that committing does not preserve unlogged-table contents across a crash. Then consider the failure of the drive or an accidental committed deletion. Close with the concrete recovery requirement: a usable backup and a tested restoration process for those failures, while retaining the stated synchronous local-commit assumptions and the base-backup/archived-WAL requirements. The existing technical distinctions should survive the rewrite.

### Figure copy

**PlanFlow introduction:** “Observed routes through the generated shop. Follow the work and the rows or prepared lookup passed to the next stage.” The sentence was expanded to fix a real semantic problem, but it now sounds like a description of a diagram contract. Prefer an explicit comparison such as “For one customer, PostgreSQL looks up ten orders and their lines. For all customers, it scans both tables and builds a hash table.” Keep the visible row counts and the distinction between the prepared lookup and emitted rows. The note that counts are not timings is useful here and can stay.

**MergeWalk introduction and note:** “the broad report's diagnostic merge plan” and “The repeated line keys must all survive” are review vocabulary. Say this is the first two orders from the merge plan and explain the duplicate behavior with the visible example: all three lines for order 1 match its order row. Preserve the many-to-many qualification as a direct statement about producing every matching pair. The three numbered instructions themselves are mostly direct.

**PageRead caption:** “The page is the unit brought into the buffer” is accurate but abstract. “PostgreSQL reads the whole page into memory, then returns P7's price” names the visible operation. Retain the ordinary page-size and illustrative placement qualification, and the possibility of reuse.

**CommitOrder body and caption:** “In this history” can become “Here.” The last step's “if that complete write survives” is necessary but awkward. Explain that recovery can use the saved page if the complete write survives the crash. Keep the caption's allowance for earlier data-page writes and its WAL-before-data condition: these are essential to the schedule's meaning, not merely defensive copy.

**CrashOutcomes caption:** “These are documented outcomes illustrated with an invented price change” belongs more naturally in provenance. Begin with the actual operating assumptions—synchronous local commit, reliable storage—and the useful comparison between B and C. Retain the fact that these are illustrative scenarios, not crash-test observations, in the article's source note. The individual cases are mostly clear and concrete already.

**IndexRoute and CompositeOrder:** The captions are comparatively direct. They explain the selected leaf, physical references and per-customer order. Do not rewrite every caption to meet a quota. “Eight invented rows make the route visible” could simply become “The selected leaf points to three rows on two table pages,” but this is lower priority than the body and execution figures.

## What should survive

The customer/date/status example, the two sorts, the concrete counts, the customer/region statistics repair, and the crash outcomes all have useful causal detail. Several passages already have a natural voice: “Now all of customer 42's entries are together,” “To total product 1, we need its contributions from all ten orders,” and “The earlier version still contains 20.00; the new one contains 22.00.” They stay with the thing being explained. The revision should develop that continuity, not turn everything into shorter sentences or remove qualifications wholesale.

## Why the first review missed this

My earlier review primarily asked whether the reader could infer the mechanism and make the promised decision. I accepted correct reasoning and a clear sequence as sufficient evidence of editorial readiness, then concentrated on visual defects. The style skill already warns that agent approval is weak evidence and that plain headings do not fix an argument. The problem was not that the skill lacked a ban on particular phrases. I did not separately assess how the prose sounded across consecutive paragraphs.

The review skill's request for consequential findings also tilted my attention toward missing explanations and wrong decisions. The present defects rarely make an isolated claim false or incomprehensible; their consequence accumulates across a page. Describing “no consequential missing prose step” as “prose ready” collapsed two different judgments. Finally, the writing reference's useful examples of repaired reasoning made it easy to compare structural completeness while paying less attention to the different rhythms of the original authors.

## Narrow instruction changes worth considering

- **Style skill:** Add a short instruction to read a section as connected prose after checking its reasoning. Ask whether sentences continue the subject or repeatedly announce the lesson, whether the same metaphor is replacing specific actions, and whether paragraph endings keep resetting the discussion. Require passage-based judgment; explicitly avoid a phrase blacklist, a sentence-length target, or a requirement that every paragraph follow one pattern.
- **Review skill:** Distinguish explanatory completeness from writing quality in the editorial assignment and verdict. Ask the reviewer to preserve representative stretches that read naturally and identify stretches that feel staged or overly formal even when understandable. A successful comprehension trace should not automatically close the writing review. Recheck revisions in surrounding paragraphs, not just as individually improved sentences.
- **Writing reference:** Record this user rejection and the resulting body revisions as evidence, with a small number of original/revised passages and why they changed. Include the failure of the previous agent-ready judgment. Do not promote the forthcoming revision into a house standard before the user has read it.
- **Authoring guidance, if it is revised:** Make the final editorial pass include figure copy and link sentences after technical repairs. Evidence and qualifications added during review should be integrated into the explanation rather than left as successive caveats. This is one bounded integration pass, not another mandatory reviewer role or scoring system.

The next useful review is the revised whole section around each repair. A collection of smoother replacement sentences can still leave the same repeated cadence or remove the reasons that made the article useful.

No further broad rewrite is warranted by this reading.** That is an editorial judgement about this revision, not a prediction of the user's approval. The prose now spends substantially more of its time explaining the database and less describing the teaching process. It keeps a technical register without the earlier report-like formality or adding a forced conversational voice.

Indexes now moves from the requested customer history to the index definition, then to the remaining date and ordering work. “The database keeps it up to date as rows change” is a direct explanation of maintenance at the point the structure is introduced. How a query runs has the most sustained improvement. Its opening explains what the execution plan contains and why the two reports might receive different plans. The nested-loop trace now ends with the match and repetition, rather than the vague “matching order's contribution.” Sorting develops in connected paragraphs: “The earlier sort handled all 30 lines…this one has only three rows to arrange.” The prose does not need another claim about what shared operation names conceal. The hash sections continue with their own operations, and the statistics section distinguishes missing distributions from a missing relationship without announcing “the useful lesson.” The final practical advice gives the reader concrete things to inspect.
