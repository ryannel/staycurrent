Sorting and aggregation
The joined rows arrive one order at a time: product 1, product 2, product 3, then the next order’s products. To total product 1, we need its quantity from all ten orders. This plan first sorts the 30 rows by product ID. All ten product-1 rows become neighbours, followed by the product-2 and product-3 rows.
GroupAggregate can now add up one product’s quantities at a time. It adds ten ones and finishes product 1 when product 2 begins; ten twos make 20, and ten threes make 30. It reads all 30 rows to produce these three totals.
The report asks for the biggest total first, so there is another Sort above the aggregate. This one orders the three totals by units sold. The earlier sort handled all 30 lines, putting each product’s quantities together so they could be added up; this one has only three rows to arrange.
PostgreSQL’s executor runs the plan by asking each operation for a row; that operation asks those beneath it for the rows it needs. But the Sort cannot supply its first row until it has read and sorted all 30. It is a blocking operation: the aggregate has to wait for it to finish. Here, “blocking” refers to waiting for input to be processed, rather than waiting for a lock.
Adding LIMIT 1 would ask for just the highest total. PostgreSQL would still need to read all the matching lines and add their quantities: the product with the largest quantity on one order need not have the largest total across all ten. The limit can reduce work on the final result, but it cannot let this plan stop after reading the first order.
