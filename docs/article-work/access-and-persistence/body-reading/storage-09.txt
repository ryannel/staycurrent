Checkpoints
As the database keeps running, it generates more WAL. Replaying all of it since the database was created would make recovery increasingly slow. A checkpoint gives recovery a more recent starting position in the log. PostgreSQL writes the dirty pages needed to ensure that all changes before that position are saved in the data files, then records that the checkpoint is complete.
Transactions continue during a checkpoint, and PostgreSQL can spread the page writes over time. After a crash, recovery reads WAL from the checkpoint’s recorded redo position to restore any later changes missing from the files. Once older log segments are no longer needed for recovery, archiving or replication, PostgreSQL can reuse them.
The price update was durable as soon as its WAL records were flushed; it did not have to wait for a checkpoint. Checkpoint frequency instead affects how much WAL a future recovery may have to replay. More frequent checkpoints can shorten recovery, at the cost of more page writes and more full-page images in the log.
