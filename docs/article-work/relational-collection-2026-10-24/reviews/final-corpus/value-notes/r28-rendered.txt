H1: Time, snapshots and distributed reads  Deep diveAbout 8 minutes AI-generated       
A distributed read needs more than a fast answer from every partition. The answers must belong to one permitted view of the database. Reading the stock before a purchase and the order after it can combine two individually valid observations into a state the purchase transaction never produced. 
A common snapshot gives all participating reads the same history boundary. The database must choose that boundary, find the versions visible there, and establish that each replica can answer it safely. Timestamps help name the boundary. Their meaning and the evidence behind them matter more than the fact that they look like times. 
We return to the purchase of order 103, with stock owned by partition A and the order and lines by B. This is the same deliberately split placement used in Transactions across partitions. The drawings use invented ordered timestamps to explain snapshots; no clock offsets, regional latency or native distributed execution are measured. 
H2: Choose one view for the read 
Before Mina’s purchase, P7 stock is 1 and order 103 is absent. After its successful transaction, stock is 0 and order 103 is pending, with its two agreed-price lines. P3 also changes from 12 to 10 in that transaction; the first drawing omits it so the two-read inconsistency is easy to see. 
Imagine a report whose first query reads stock on A. The purchase then commits, and the report’s second query reads orders on B. If each query chooses a new current view, the report can see stock 1 and order 103 present. Atomic commit has not failed: the queries asked about different moments.

On one PostgreSQL server, Read Committed can give separate statements separate snapshots, while a Repeatable Read transaction keeps its established view. Across partitions the same requirement needs a distributed implementation. The system must carry the read’s chosen timestamp or equivalent snapshot identity to every participant, rather than letting each independently substitute whatever is newest there. 
This is separate from whether concurrent writing decisions are serializable. A coherent old report can be perfectly consistent while too stale for a purchase decision. A stable snapshot does not reserve stock, and a historical observation of stock 1 does not authorise another sale. 
H2: A timestamp names a version boundary 
With multi-version concurrency control, the database retains versions of rows. A read at timestamp T selects versions visible at that boundary. If the purchase’s commit is ordered at 110, a snapshot at 100 excludes it and one at 115 includes it, provided unresolved transaction state is handled correctly. All related effects use the transaction’s commit interpretation. 
“Take the latest version whose timestamp is no greater than T” describes only part of the job. The reader also needs to know whether a provisional write at or below T will commit, and whether missing replication could later supply a qualifying version. Returning an old row merely because a newer one has not arrived is not proof that the old row is the correct answer at T. 
Distributed systems use different protocols to establish that knowledge. They may consult a write authority, wait for a replica to apply enough history, resolve transaction records or use a published safe historical frontier. A numeric timestamp does not replace those mechanisms.

Read the four cases
At 100, both replicas can answer even while B is safe only through 105: stock 1, no order 103. At 115, A can answer stock 0 but B cannot safely complete its portion while safe only through 105. Once B is safe through 120, the combined answer at 115 is stock 0 and pending order 103. Catching B up does not change the chosen answer at 100.    
At 100, both replicas could answer a complete older view. At 115, A could select the new stock, but B could not safely certify its portion. The correct result was to withhold completion, wait or route appropriately. Using B’s latest available old value would have manufactured a mixed snapshot. 
Letting B catch up made 115 answerable. It did not change the answer at 100. The chosen view and the replica’s ability to serve it are two separate inputs. That distinction is why a consistent historical read can be useful near the reader even while a fresh read needs another path. 
H2: Physical clocks do not give perfect order 
A machine’s physical clock estimates wall time. Two machines can disagree, drift or be adjusted. If A stamps a sent message 100 and B’s slower clock stamps its receipt 95, sorting those raw readings would place the receipt first. The message itself proves the opposite order. 
A logical clock names order without claiming exact elapsed time. One simple scheme increments a local counter for events and, on receiving a message, moves past both the local counter and the timestamp carried by the message. A receipt can then have logical timestamp 101 even if the physical clock reads 95. 
That gives one direction of reasoning: if one event causally precedes another, its logical timestamp comes first. The reverse does not follow. Two events that never communicated can still receive different numbers, and those numbers alone do not prove which caused which. Nor does a gap of 10 logical ticks mean ten milliseconds. 
A hybrid logical clock combines a physical-time component with a logical component. It can retain a useful relationship to wall time while moving forward when messages reveal a later timestamp. This is useful for version ordering, but it does not make the underlying physical clocks exact or eliminate all uncertainty from a fresh read. 
CockroachDB uses hybrid logical clocks and an uncertainty mechanism around reads that could encounter writes from a node with a faster clock. Such reads can need a timestamp adjustment and retry or refresh under the engine’s protocol. Clock-offset limits are correctness assumptions to operate within, not performance knobs to relax without understanding the guarantee. 
H2: Uncertainty can be part of the contract 
Another approach exposes an interval within which the actual time is believed to lie. If one event’s possible interval overlaps another’s, their physical-clock readings alone do not prove a real-time order. The system must account for that overlap rather than silently treating the centre of each interval as exact. 
Spanner’s TrueTime API supplies bounded time uncertainty, and its commit protocol includes waiting to establish the required real-time ordering. At its Serializable level, Spanner’s external-consistency guarantee relates transaction order to the order clients observe successful transactions: a transaction that starts after another has finished must not appear before it. This is stronger than merely finding some serial order. 
That does not mean every distributed database waits in exactly this way, or that a server with ordinary clock synchronisation automatically offers the same contract. Clock source, uncertainty bounds, timestamp assignment, concurrency control and commit protocol form one design. Compare the documented promises of the actual system. 
Keep application times separate too. The shop can record when a price was intended to take effect and when a correction was entered. Those are business-history facts. An effective date earlier than another row’s date is not evidence that the database committed it earlier. A catalogue timestamp supplied by an application should not be treated as a distributed commit certificate. 
H2: Why an old read can be safe 
A replica can often serve history more easily than an arbitrarily fresh view. Once a protocol has established that no future accepted write will take a timestamp at or below a boundary, and the replica has applied the required records, the answer for that boundary is fixed. It need not discover the current state of every writer for each such read. 
CockroachDB calls one part of this mechanism a closed timestamp. It constrains future writes for a range. A follower also needs the corresponding replicated state applied and enough retained versions. A closed timestamp received without its required state is not, by itself, an answerable snapshot. 
For a read spanning ranges, every participating range must satisfy the chosen timestamp. Different ranges can be at different safe frontiers, so the slower one constrains where the complete local historical read can finish. A newer read may wait or be routed to a replica with the needed state; it must not silently become an older read unless that is the API’s documented contract. 
“Follower read” also does not universally mean “stale”. Products can provide strong reads from suitable followers under additional protocols and placement choices. CockroachDB distinguishes strong reads for global tables from stale historical follower reads. CockroachDB isolation and follower reads develops the native modes and their restrictions. 
H2: Choose the freshness the operation needs 
An exact historical timestamp fixes one view. A bounded-staleness request instead allows a range of acceptable timestamps, so the database can choose a suitable one within the bound. A strong current read asks for a stronger relationship to completed writes. The application should state which promise makes its answer useful.

Read-your-writes is not implied by consistency alone. A historical snapshot from before order 103 is coherent and still omits Mina’s purchase. Use the system’s supported way to carry the dependency forward, or route to an authority that can honour it. Do not invent a timestamp from the browser’s clock and assume it orders after the server’s commit. 
The timestamp’s scope also matters for pagination. Choosing “recent enough” independently for each page can let the view change between pages. Reuse an appropriate snapshot identity for the complete export if that is the contract. Pagination and consistent exports follows its lifetime and continuation boundary. 
H2: History and availability have limits 
Versions cannot be retained for free forever. Garbage collection eventually removes older history. A replica may have applied the latest data yet no longer retain the version needed for a very old timestamp. Being ahead of T and retaining the answer at T are distinct conditions. 
A long snapshot or protected timestamp can require retaining versions, increasing storage and cleanup work. A historical-read API may instead reject requests older than its retention window. Plan an export’s maximum lifetime, retry behaviour and cleanup alongside its chosen view. 
Freshness and availability also separate during a network break. An isolated replica may still answer a certified old snapshot from retained local data. It cannot conclude from the silence that no new commits occurred elsewhere. Serving history is not acquiring new write authority or proving the current global state. 
Check these behaviours with labelled transactions and their identities, not only clock differences. Verify that one common snapshot includes both effects or neither, that a lagging replica waits, routes or rejects as documented, and that a read after an acknowledged write includes it when promised. Record actual chosen timestamps, safe/applied progress and retention settings when the engine exposes them. Several local processes can test a mechanism; they cannot measure regional latency or prove independent failures. 
The practical question is therefore twofold: which history does this operation need, and what evidence lets these replicas answer that history? Once those are explicit, locality and latency become costs you can compare without quietly changing the meaning of the result.
