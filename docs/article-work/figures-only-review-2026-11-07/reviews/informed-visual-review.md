## Ranked findings

1. **Medium — Source admission and cache-outage behaviour are absent from the visual explanation.** The brief centres on keeping useful requests inside source capacity and choosing what others receive. Figure 1 names 40 slots, and Figure 5 assumes 2,000 fills/s, but neither shows occupied places, bounded waiting, admission/refusal, or the fallback path. There is no transition from same-key calls to many distinct misses, nor from Redis hits to failed cache calls that still face the source limit. The independent reader consequently could not explain excess-work handling. This leaves the main reasoning in questions 6 and 9 entirely to prose, including why a start-rate limit or large per-instance connection pool is insufficient and why fleet growth changes the aggregate allowance.

2. **Medium — The sequence omits the failed-recovery feedback loop and the decision to reduce or increase demand.** Figure 5 shows successful recovery under an already-held fill allowance. It does not show overload slowing fills, few completed fills leaving the cache cold, retries multiplying attempts, or abandoned queries retaining capacity. Figure 3 teaches one lingering query, but does not connect it to this system-wide loop. Nothing visual explains why restored Redis or ordinary traffic levels may not restore service, why retry attempts share the same scarce allowance, or why the ramp must follow source latency and executing work. These are central to questions 7 and 10 and the operational part of question 8, not omitted implementation detail.

3. **Medium — The choice between local sharing and a fleet-wide lease is not taught.** Figure 2 proves that callers can share one load; Figure 3 proves that a lease can expire before work ends. The bridge between them is absent: separate processes can each have a shared fill, and remaining duplicate cost determines whether a lease is worthwhile. The cheap P7 card versus expensive list comparison and the 20-instance scope came only from prose. A reader can select the independent caller budget from the visuals, but cannot defend adding or refusing a lease as question 2 requires. The efficiency-versus-value-correctness distinction is also not established by the refusal label alone.

4. **Medium — Refresh scheduling choices remain outside the figures.** Figure 4 clearly teaches the hard-limit rule, but depicts a single refresh at a given time. The sequence does not explain request-driven probabilistic selection, why duplicate attempts can remain, how jitter spreads different keys rather than callers of one key, or why neither scheduling method helps an empty cache. Those are the consequential choices in question 5. The formula and exact simulation derivation may stay in prose; the policy distinctions are central reasoning.

5. **Medium — Figure 5 shows the recovery advantage without showing its cause.** Its labels and caption establish the compared policies and assumption, and the graph proves which result is better in this model. My first account supplied the popularity mechanism from prior knowledge. The figure does not make visible that a small useful set serves many requests, or that an arbitrary catalogue order spends the same fill allowance differently. Consequently it supports choosing between these plotted outcomes more strongly than transferring that decision to a different warming order. This is narrower than the failed-recovery gap above.

6. **Low — Figure 2's “waits” labels briefly obscure allowance versus elapsed time.** The first account initially read “waits 150 ms” as duration, then resolved it using the shorter solid lines, dotted deadlines and caption. The final understanding was correct, so this is a small reading cost rather than false visual understanding.

No separate finding is raised merely because the figures omit the in-flight map's data structure, Lua script, exact XFetch formula, database commands or every observed experimental count. Nor does the absence of a new map itself count as a defect.
