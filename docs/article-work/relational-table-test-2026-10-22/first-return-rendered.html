Relational databases

GuideAbout 2 minutes AI-generated

A relational database keeps an application's data in tables and lets the application read and change it. A shop needs to remember who its customers are, what they have ordered and which orders have been paid for. Those facts have to outlast a visit to its website: Mina can close her browser today and come back to her order tomorrow.

Keeping the facts is only part of the job. The shop also needs answers from them. Mina wants to see her orders; someone packing an order needs to know who placed it. A relational database can put customers and orders in separate tables, then combine their information when a question needs both. The application asks for the answer it needs from the stored data.

This collection follows a shop selling tea and tea things. We start with how to read its data, before writing the instructions that ask the database for it.

Tables, rows and columns

A table is a named set of rows and columns. Each row holds one entry; each column holds one kind of fact about those entries. The shop's customers table has one row per customer and two columns: customer_id, the number that identifies the customer, and name, their name.

Read Mina's row across: her customer_id is 1 and her name is Mina Shah. Read the name column down and you get all three names. Each row has the same columns, so Tariq's name occupies the same column as Mina's.

The customer number distinguishes one customer from another. Two people could both be called Mina Shah; the shop would give them different customer numbers. The 1 identifies Mina even if she later changes her name.

The orders table has one row per order. Its order_id identifies the order, and its customer_id says which customer placed it. Figure 2 leaves out the other order details so we can follow that connection.

Who placed order 103? Find 103 in the order_id column and read across: its customer_id is 1. Now find 1 in the customers table's customer_id column. That row gives the name Mina Shah. The shared value connects the order to its customer.

Order 101 also carries customer number 1, so both orders belong to Mina. Order 102 carries 2 and belongs to Tariq. Edda has a customer row but no order row referring to her: the shop can know a customer before they place an order.

The connection comes from the values, not where the rows sit on the page. Rearranging either table would leave order 103 belonging to customer 1. And the two identifiers do different jobs: 103 names an order; 1 names its customer.

Sources: PostgreSQL's Concepts and Table Basics explain rows and columns; Joins Between Tables explains combining rows by matching values. The shop tables are an invented example.
