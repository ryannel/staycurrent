## Claims that hold at the commissioned level

Tables, rows, columns, explicitly chosen identity, and composite keys are explained coherently. The table caption explicitly distinguishes content from disk layout. The prose does not imply that every table has an automatically assigned ID, that a table is one file, or that a relational interface forces a row store. PostgreSQL's [table basics](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/ddl-basics.html) and [relational concepts](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/tutorial-concepts.html) support the logical description.

The relationships discussion correctly treats shared current facts and historical purchase facts as modelling decisions. It does not imply that a foreign key retrieves values or that a join requires a declared foreign key. The join explanation and the separation of a requested result from an execution strategy agree with PostgreSQL's [join tutorial](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/tutorial-join.html) and [planner description](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/planner-optimizer.html). There is no promised query plan, measured speedup, or claim that the planner always finds the best plan.

The index section states a conditional benefit, explains access-pattern dependence, allows a full scan to be preferable, and gives the write-maintenance cost. Those are supported by the [index introduction](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/indexes-intro.html). The constraints section distinguishes declared database rules from a complete business invariant. Requiring a customer ID as well as its matching reference avoids treating an optional foreign key as a required relationship. The enforcement caveat in M1 is the remaining gap.

The transaction explanation gives atomicity an appropriate boundary: the operations placed inside the database transaction, with application responsibility for choosing those operations and handling errors. It does not promise rollback of an external charge, email, or shipment. The subsequent concurrency section makes clear that atomicity alone does not prevent conflicting decisions. These distinctions agree with PostgreSQL's [transaction tutorial](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/tutorial-transactions.html) and [isolation chapter](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/transaction-iso.html). Omitting a durability and recovery survey is consistent with the bounded commission; the article does not make an unqualified crash-survival promise.

The lock description says a writer *can* wait, not that every read is blocked or every race is prevented. It also explains the cost of holding up other work. Multiple versions are presented as an approach used by many engines, and isolation guarantees are explicitly said to vary. This agrees with [row-level locking](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/explicit-locking.html#LOCKING-ROWS) and [MVCC](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/mvcc-intro.html). The final paragraph correctly separates a rejected transaction from an unknown outcome after connection loss; it does not recommend blind retries for both.

SQLite's in-process deployment is supported by its [serverless explanation](https://www.sqlite.org/serverless.html). The overlap with documents, text search, and vector retrieval is appropriately conditional: PostgreSQL's [JSON documentation](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/datatype-json.html), [text-search introduction](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/textsearch-intro.html), and [pgvector's project documentation](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector) establish the capabilities. The article does not confuse an extension with a core feature or claim specialist-system equivalence.
