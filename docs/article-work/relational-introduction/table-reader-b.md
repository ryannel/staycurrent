# First reading of the products table

I inspected only `table-light.html` and `table-dark.html` at `http://127.0.0.1:4325/` before writing this interpretation. I have not yet read the revised article, brief, evidence, or another review. I used rendered screenshots and accessibility text, and left the viewport unchanged. I had previously reviewed the separate relationships and query aids, so the product names were familiar; the observations below concern what this new aid itself supplies.

The aid teaches what a row and column mean through a small product catalogue. One row describes product P7: a blue mug, priced at €20, with five in stock. The other describes P8: a bowl, priced at €24, with three in stock. Reading across gives the facts about one product; reading down a column compares the same kind of fact across products. The opening sentence makes that distinction explicit.

I interpreted the price as the price of one product, rather than the combined value of the stock. That is the ordinary reading of a products table with separate Price and In stock columns, although the visible heading does not explicitly say “per item.” I read P7 and P8 as identifiers, not row numbers or quantities. I did not infer that the identifiers must be consecutive or that row order matters.

The reading order is straightforward: title, explanation, headings, two rows. Row separators make it easy to keep each value with its product. There are no arrows, colours, or symbols whose meaning needs a legend. The euro sign and the word “stock” have familiar meanings. Nothing looks editable, sortable, or clickable. “Example table” sets the expectation of a static aid.

The labels and content are sufficient to teach this narrow idea without article prose. The aid does not explain relationships between tables, uniqueness requirements, data types, or disk layout, and I did not read it as making claims about those matters. There is considerable empty space to the right of the short table, but it does not interfere with the lesson. The full aid fits in the supplied viewport.

The same interpretation holds in the dark theme. Headings, values, and row boundaries remain readable. I found no material ambiguity in either rendering.
