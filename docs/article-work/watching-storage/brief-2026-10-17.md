# Watching storage in a running database: brief, 17 October 2026

Companion to `relational/storage.astro` (Storage and durability). Page:
`src/pages/learn/databases/relational/watching-storage.astro`. Registered in
`src/lib/relational-collection.ts` (storage group, status "draft"); that file is
not edited by this work.

## Reader and job

A working backend engineer on PostgreSQL, MySQL (InnoDB) or SQLite who has read, or
already understands, Storage and durability: pages, the buffer pool, dirty pages,
the log and LSNs, checkpoints, flushes, row versions. They are now on call for a
database. They need to know which numbers and log lines show each mechanism at
work, what an ordinary reading looks like and what a bad one looks like, and how to
work from a symptom (slow commits, a long restart, a filling log directory, a table
or undo history that keeps growing) to the mechanism behind it.

Central question: *each storage mechanism leaves traces in a running server; how do
you read them, and what do they tell you in an incident?*

Useful stopping point: the reader can open the right view or log for each mechanism
in their engine, turn cumulative counters into rates, recognise the normal shape of
a checkpoint cycle, and follow the four incidents to a cause.

Opening: start from the mechanisms the reader already knows and the fact that they
are invisible from the application (a commit is only "slow"), then say what this
article adds: where each mechanism reports itself. Not a list of contents.

## Topic tree and depth decisions

Derived from the subject and the reader before looking at an outline.

1. **How to watch at all** (method). Needs: where each engine reports (PostgreSQL
   cumulative statistics views and the server log; InnoDB status variables,
   `INNODB_METRICS`, `SHOW ENGINE INNODB STATUS`, the error log; SQLite has no server,
   so PRAGMAs, file sizes and the C API from inside the application); counters are
   cumulative, so sample twice and divide; statistics reset (PostgreSQL after a crash;
   MySQL status at restart); "normal" means your own baseline, not a published
   number. Depth: one short section, ~300 words. No figure.
2. **Cache reads and hits.** Needs: hit ratio from deltas; where a "read" may still be
   an OS cache hit (PostgreSQL); InnoDB waits for a free page; SQLite per connection.
   Abnormal: reads per second rising at the same throughput. Depth: short (~250 words);
   sibling owns the rest. Link to The buffer pool.
3. **Dirty pages and who writes them.** Parts: dirty count (PostgreSQL via
   `pg_buffercache_summary()`; InnoDB `Innodb_buffer_pool_pages_dirty`); the three
   writers (checkpointer, background writer, backends; InnoDB page cleaners, LRU
   flushing); why a backend write is the bad sign; InnoDB free-page waits. Depth:
   moderate (~450 words), real numbers from the local run in prose and a small table.
4. **Checkpoints: frequency, duration, cause.** Parts: reading the PostgreSQL
   checkpoint log line field by field; timed vs requested (`pg_stat_checkpointer`);
   the "too frequently" warning; the spread write and its duration; full-page images
   after each checkpoint start as the visible cost; InnoDB checkpoint age against
   redo capacity and what happens near full; SQLite checkpoint result columns.
   Depth: substantial, the core of the article (~900 words, h3s). Figure: a real
   recorded checkpoint cycle (WAL rate and dirty buffers over 120 s, checkpoint
   windows shaded). A two-run comparison (1 GB vs 64 MB `max_wal_size`) shown as a
   small table with honest limits.
5. **Log volume and retention.** Parts: measuring the rate (LSN difference;
   `pg_stat_wal`; `Innodb_redo_log_current_lsn` / `Innodb_os_log_written`); what drives
   it (full-page images, indexes, bulk changes); why space is not freed: checkpoint
   not yet run, archiving failing, replication slot, `wal_keep_size`; InnoDB redo is
   fixed capacity, the binary log is what grows (expiry; replicas not tracked);
   SQLite WAL growth from readers or disabled checkpoints; long transactions do not
   hold PostgreSQL WAL (they hold row versions). Depth: substantial (~700 words, two
   h3s), real archive-failure and slot outputs.
6. **Flush and commit latency.** Parts: commit latency floor equals log flush time;
   PostgreSQL `pg_stat_io` (object `wal`) writes/fsyncs/times and the
   `wal_sync_method` subtlety; wait events `WalSync`/`WalWrite`; `wal_buffers_full`;
   InnoDB `Innodb_os_log_fsyncs`, `Innodb_log_waits`, Performance Schema redo file
   I/O; SQLite: time commits in the application. Depth: moderate (~450 words).
7. **Old-version history.** Parts: PostgreSQL dead tuples and the oldest snapshot
   (link row-versions); InnoDB history list length and the oldest transaction;
   SQLite WAL held by a reader (real run). Depth: moderate (~450 words), real SQLite
   output.
8. **Startup and recovery log lines.** Parts: real PostgreSQL crash-restart log with
   each line explained; how long and what decides it; statistics reset; progress
   lines for a long replay; InnoDB's documented messages, including rollback
   progress; SQLite's recovery notice through `sqlite3_log`. Depth: moderate to
   substantial (~550 words) with a real annotated log.
9. **Worked incident readings.** Four incidents named in the commission: slow
   commits; a long restart; a growing log directory; a table or undo history that
   keeps growing. Each: what to look at first, what distinguishes the causes, what
   to change. Depth: substantial (~900 words, one h3 each). Reuses earlier signals,
   adds the reasoning order.
10. **Summary table of signals per engine.** One table, signal × PostgreSQL ×
    InnoDB × SQLite, with "what a bad reading suggests". Placed last before sources
    as a reference.

## Left out on purpose

- Cache sizing, eviction, warm-up: sibling `buffer-pool`.
- Per-query diagnosis and lock waits: `slow-queries`.
- Replication lag beyond its effect on log retention: `replicas-and-failover`.
- Alert thresholds as numbers: there is no universal value; the article teaches
  baselines and shapes.
- Monitoring products and exporters: tool-specific, change often.
- OS-level disk metrics beyond one sentence (iostat await) and table bloat measurement.

## Evidence approach

- Every view, column, variable, PRAGMA and log line verified against PostgreSQL 18,
  MySQL 8.4 and SQLite documentation, or the PostgreSQL 18.6 binary and source;
  recorded in `evidence.md`.
- PostgreSQL outputs come from real local runs (PostgreSQL 18.6, macOS laptop),
  labelled as such. SQLite outputs from Python's bundled SQLite 3.54.0.
- No MySQL server was available locally; InnoDB items are documentation-verified
  only, and the article shows no InnoDB output.
